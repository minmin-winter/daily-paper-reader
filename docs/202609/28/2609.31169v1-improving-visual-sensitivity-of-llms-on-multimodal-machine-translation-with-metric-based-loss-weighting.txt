Abstract 

Multimodal Machine Translation aims to in-corporate additional signal from non-textual modalities to improve translations by resolv-ing ambiguities. While models, through mul-timodal fusion, are able to accept images re-lated to the source text, they can ignore this information. Therefore, increasing their vi-sual sensitivity remains an active research area. In this work, we introduce a training method, Metric-based Loss Weighting, that improves visual grounding of translations by increasing the loss function for tokens that benefit from the accompanying image. We identify these tokens using the Point-wise Cross-mutual In-formation (PCXMI) metric, which compares the model’s output probabilities with and with-out visual context. We introduce a Congruency-based PCXMI metric and experimentally show that both metrics working in combination yield the best results. We evaluate our method by fine-tuning three pretrained Multimodal Large Language Models on the task of Image-guided Machine Translation for three language direc-tions. Metric-based Loss Weighting outper-forms other tested methods on the CoMMuTE contrastive dataset, improving accuracy by up to more than 7 percentage points compared to standard fine-tuning, while maintaining strong general translation performance. 

1 Introduction 

Sentence-level Machine Translation (MT) systems achieved high performance (Vaswani et al., 2017; Hassan et al., 2018; Costa-jussà et al., 2022; Tiede-mann et al., 2024), but they are inherently lim-ited by the lack of context. In Multimodal Ma-chine Translation (MMT), the additional signal from other modalities can improve translation in cases where text alone is ambiguous. This signal can be audio, video, or image (Sulubacak et al., 2020; Garg et al., 2022; Feng et al., 2025). In this study, we concentrate our efforts on Image-based MT. While the images are available to the MMT sys-tems, the reliance on the other modality is not guaranteed. Indeed, Elliott (2018) did not find the expected performance drop of the tested sys-tems after randomly replacing images. Wu et al. (2021) attributed some gains of the MMT systems to regularization. Therefore, visual sensitivity of the MMT models has seen increased attention from researchers in recent years (Sato et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2023; Cheng et al., 2024; Bowen et al., 2024; Futeral et al., 2025). As traditional translation metrics such as BLEU (Papineni et al., 2002) are not sensitive enough to judge disambiguations, we turn towards CoMMuTE (Futeral et al., 2023). It is a contrastive dataset where the tested models are presented with multiple possible translations of the ambiguous source sentence and tasked with selecting the cor-rect one. The selection is based on the probability that models give to each option, and the final metric is the accuracy of assigning the highest probability to the correct one. The field of MMT went from training special-ized architectures from random initialization (Yin et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021) to using pre-trained models as building blocks and fine-tuning the re-sulting model with multimodal parallel data (Gupta et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2025). Recently, more fo-cus has been given to Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) (Alayrac et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023; Laurençon et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2025) due to their generalizability and strong performance in different tasks (Zhu et al., 2024). In this study, we concentrate on methods that could be used to en-hance fine-tuning of MLLMs on the task of MMT to increase the visual grounding of the translation. To mitigate this issue, we propose a training strategy where we identify the tokens in the target sequence that benefit from context and, subsequently, increase the loss for those tokens during training. To identify the image-dependent tokens, we use the Point-wise Cross-Mutual Information (PCXMI) metric (Fernandes et al., 2023), which compares the output proba-bilities of the model in two situations: with and without the context (see Figure 1). We propose a modification to this metric by using an incongruent image as context instead of the lack of it. We com-bine both metrics to achieve the best results. We test our method against several techniques adapted to fine-tuning of MLLMs using three models and three language directions. Our Metric-based Loss Weighting method outperforms the baselines in terms of CoMMuTE accuracy on most of the tested scenarios. It improves the accuracy by up to more than 7 percentage points compared to the standard fine-tuning. Our contributions are as follows: 1. We introduce a training strategy for fine-tuning MLLMs that improves their visual sensitivity on the task of MMT based on identifying and increas-ing loss function for image-dependent tokens. 2. We adapt the Point-wise Cross-Mutual Informa-tion (PCXMI) metric (Fernandes et al., 2023) to MMT, introduce Congruency-based PCXMI met-ric, and experimentally show that both metrics working in combination yield best results. 3. We adapt and evaluate several existing meth-ods for fine-tuning MLLMs on the task of MMT, establishing the baseline for future research. 4. We show that the pre-trained models exhibit high visual sensitivity which can be further in-creased with specialized fine-tuning techniques. 5. We empirically confirm the discrepancy be-tween traditional translation metrics such as BLEU (Papineni et al., 2002) and the image re-liance of the MLLMs. 

2 Related Work 

Researchers have been interested in incorporating the information coming from other modalities to enhance text-to-text translation (Sulubacak et al., 2020; Garg et al., 2022; Feng et al., 2025). While this includes audio and video (Gu et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023), large focus has been concentrated on single images related to the source text (Elliott et al., 2017; Barrault et al., 2018; Cheng et al., 2024; Futeral et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025). The methods proposed specialized architectures concen-trating on feature extraction (Yao and Wan, 2020; Fang and Feng, 2022), cross-modal fusion and alignment (Zhao et al., 2022; Guo et al., 2022), and cross-modal attention (Nishihara et al., 2020; Gupta et al., 2023). Other notable directions include: employing aux-iliary tasks, such as Visually Conditioned Masked Language Modeling (VMLM) (Futeral et al., 2023, 2025), image captioning (Gupta et al., 2023), or visual question answering (Zhang et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025), masking the source sentence to in-crease visual grounding (Sato et al., 2023; Cheng 2et al., 2024; Bowen et al., 2024), and data aug-mentation by image generation (Long et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022c; Chen et al., 2025), using text-to-image models (Goodfellow et al., 2014; Rombach et al., 2022), or backtranslation (Li et al., 2021). The MMT models are often trained on the widely used Multi30k dataset and evaluated on the accom-panying testsets (Elliott et al., 2016, 2017; Barrault et al., 2018). Although the systems show increased BLEU scores (Papineni et al., 2002) compared to the text-only baselines, the benefits have been at-tributed to regularization (Wu et al., 2021). For example, Elliott (2018), after replacing the relevant images with random ones, did not see the expected drop in performance. This could be caused by the insufficient number of examples in the Multi30k training dataset that require context in the form of the accompanying image for correct transla-tion (Futeral et al., 2023). Earlier works trained models from random ini-tialization (Yin et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021), but in-creasingly often researchers utilize pretrained mod-els (Gupta et al., 2023; Futeral et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025). This transition is motivated by the strong generalization capabilities of the pretrained models, acquired through training on large amounts of data. The popular models include CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) and BLIP (Li et al., 2022b) for image feature extraction, and Large Language Models, such as mBART (Liu et al., 2020) or Qwen (Yang et al., 2025), for text understanding and generation. The emergence of strong general-purpose Multi-modal Large Language Models (MLLMs), capable of incorporating visual information without archi-tectural modifications, presents a potential shift in the approach to Multimodal Machine Transla-tion (Alayrac et al., 2022; Awadalla et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024). While Xiong and Zhao (2025) and Yu et al. (2025) evaluated their methods against MLLMs, and Zhu et al. (2024) performed a large scale evaluation of publicly-available MLLMs, we concentrate on instruction-tuning (Wei et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2026; Hu et al., 2022) and evaluating the open-weights mod-els (Laurençon et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2025). Our method is inspired by Metric-based Fine-tuning and Loss Weighting, the two separate tech-niques introduced for encoder-decoder models by M ˛ aka et al. (2025) for the task of Context-aware MT. Importantly, both techniques used only a tex-tual modality, and the loss weighting was based on the external annotations. In this work, we combine both techniques, and adapt it to the multimodal setting of Image-guided MT. Furthermore, we in-troduce a new Metric that uses incongruent images instead of not providing an image. 

3 Method 

Our proposed method consists of two steps. First, we identify which tokens in the target sentence re-quire context. To do this, we measure how much the output probabilities of the pretrained model change when the context image is present or not (alternatively, when the image is not related to the text). Second, we use this information during train-ing to improve the visual sensitivity of the model by increasing the loss corresponding to the context-dependent tokens. Similar to M ˛ aka et al. (2025), we employ the Point-wise Cross-Mutual Information (PCXMI) metric Fernandes et al. (2023) for the identifica-tion step. The original PCXMI metric compares the model’s output probabilities with and without previous sentences as context. We adapt it to the setting of Image-guided MT by treating the accom-panying image as context. The equation below calculates the P CXM I for the j-th token in the target sequence:

This means that we bias the model towards the tokens for which it already exhibits strong visual sensitivity. 

4 Experiments 

We experimentally evaluated the effects of our method on fine-tuning MLLMs on the task of MMT. We are specifically interested in observing how it affects the visual sensitivity of the tested models. We experimented with three popular open-weights instruction-tuned models: Qwen3-VL 4B Instruct 1, Qwen3-VL 8B Instruct 2 (Bai et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025), and IDEFICS 9B Instruct 3 (Laurençon et al., 2023). We used LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) to fine-tune the models on the Multi30k dataset (Elliott et al., 2016). We experimented with three language directions: English-to-German, English-to-French, and English-to-Czech. We compared our method with the following baselines: • Fine-tuning - simply fine-tuning the model on the Image-guided MT training dataset. • Visually Conditioned Masked Language Modeling (VMLM) - fine-tuning with the aux-iliary task, where the model is presented with the source sentence containing masked tokens, and it has to predict those masked tokens (Futeral et al., 2023, 2025). We mask words with the probability p,which is a hyper-parameter, with a string MASK{n} ,where n goes from 1 to the total number of masked words in the source text. Because VMLM does not require a parallel corpus, often an additional multimodal dataset in the source language is used. In our setting, we use only the Multi30k dataset, discarding the target-side sentences, to keep the methods using comparable amounts of data. • Metric-based Fine-tuning - we adapt this method from the original text-only version (M ˛ aka et al., 2025). After the initial fine-tuning, we cal-culate the PCXMI and CPCXMI metrics and se-lect top-k examples with the highest maximum values of the calculated metrics for further fine-tuning. We take the maximum of the two metrics, as this yielded better results in the preliminary ex-periments than either of the metrics on their own. • Word Dropout - dropping random tokens from the source sentence to force the model to use the image to improve translation, adapted to Image-guided Machine Translation from CoWord Dropout (Fernandes et al., 2021). The probability of dropping out a token is controlled by the hyper-parameter p. In the preliminary experiments, we determined that dropout on the word-level was bet-ter than token-level. Therefore, we drop out words based on the Moses Tokenizer 4(Koehn et al., 2007).

5 Analysis 

In this section, we take a deeper look at the PCXMI and CPCXMI distributions. Next, we examine translation examples produced by our method and standard fine-tuning. Lastly, we experiment with combining our method with Word Dropout, another technique that achieves strong results. Additionally, in Appendix B we present the ablation study. 

5.1 Metrics Analysis 

In Figure 2, we present two examples of the per-token values of the PCXMI and CPCXMI met-rics. Further examples can be found in Appendix C. While both metrics are capable of identifying the tokens that require image for disambiguation, they are not perfect, and can ignore or misidentify those tokens. This can be partially mitigated by taking the maximum of both metrics. In Appendix C, we present the distributions of the calculated metrics (PCXMI, CPCXMI, and their maximum) on the training subset of the Multi30k dataset for each tested model and lan-guage direction. The values of the metrics depend heavily on the model, as can be seen in the dif-ference between both the Qwen3-VL models and IDEFICS 9B. The difference between language directions for the same model is a lot smaller. Ad-ditionally, all distributions show a concentration of values close to zero and a long tail at high values. This means that most examples in the dataset do not require, according to the tested models, images to disambiguate. The distributions for Multi30k and CoMMute datasets are compared in Appendix C, where we show that the distribution for the latter is denser for higher values of the metric. 

5.2 Qualitative Analysis 

In this section, we investigate the drop in BLEU exhibited by Metric-based Loss Weighting com-pared to Fine-tuning seen for Qwen3-VL 8B on the English-to-French language direction. We perform the qualitative analysis of the translations of the MSCOCO testset, where the difference in BLEU between the methods is the largest. In Figure 3, we present the distributions of per-example BLEU scores for Fine-tuning and our method. They differ mostly in two regions: (1) BLEU scores around 100, where Fine-tuning has more examples, (2) BLEU scores around 30, where the Weighting dis-tribution is denser. We examined the examples that exhibit the high-est difference in BLEU between Fine-tuning and our method (both ways, with both Fine-tuning and our method achieving better scores). We found that the largest drop in example-level BLEU scores mostly corresponds to acceptable translations that differ from the gold target translation. More exam-ples with our descriptions and further details of the analysis are presented in Appendix D.

6 Conclusions 

This work introduced a training strategy for fine-tuning MLLMs for the task of MMT that increases the visual grounding during translation. It in-creases the loss for the tokens that benefit from context in the form of the image, as measured by the Point-wise Cross-Mutual Information (PCXMI) and Congruency-based PCXMI (CPCXMI). Both metrics rely on the model’s output probabilities with and without the image (or with incongruent image in case of CPCXMI). We empirically tested our technique against ex-isting methods adapted for the task of fine-tuning MLLMs on three models and three language di-rections, and showed its increased performance 8in terms of CoMMuTE accuracy, while maintain-ing strong overall translation quality, as measured by BLEU and COMET. Our results confirm that general-purpose translation metrics (BLEU and COMET) fail to capture how strongly models rely on visual modality for MMT. In future work, we will explore how the metrics can inform masking and word dropout on the source side. 

7 Limitations 

While we investigate three language directions and three sub-families, all of them come from the Indo-European family, and all use the Latin script. This limitation was imposed by the language directions covered by both Multi30k and CoMMuTE datasets. We suspect that the measured performance of the methods goes beyond the tested language pairs, but this claim has not been tested experimentally. Due to the low number of repeated runs and the vari-ability of the measured metrics, the results of the top methods are not different with statistical sig-nificance. We limited our investigation to only the same multimodal parallel dataset for each method, while VMLM could use additional multimodal monolingual data. For Metric-based Loss Weighting, we calculate the image dependence metrics using the baseline models. Updating the metrics during training or using a separate (presumably more visually sen-sitive) model could yield different results, but we did not test this setting. Furthermore, we limit our method to PCXMI and CPCXMI, and a broader ex-ploration of image dependence metrics could lead to increased performance. Finally, while we tested the methods on three MLLMs, their sizes were limited to less than 10B parameters.

A Training Details 

We trained the models using the AdamW opti-mizer (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019). We used a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 24GB for Qwen3-VL 4B, and a single NVIDIA A100 40GB for Qwen3-VL 8B and IDEFICS 9B. The average training times per epoch for each model are pre-sented in Table 5. The time for Metric-based Fine-tuning is shown only for the second stage training (training on the top-k examples). The training time for VMLM is roughly doubled compared to fine-tuning, because of the auxiliary task of masked 12 language modeling. The times for other meth-ods are comparable, meaning that additional pre-processing (dropout, masking) and post-processing (loss weighting) do not meaningfully increase the training time compared to basic fine-tuning. The training was performed on the Multi30k dataset and evaluated on its testsets and the CoMMuTE contrastive dataset. The sizes of the used datasets can be found in Table 6.
