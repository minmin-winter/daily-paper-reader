Abstract 

Extending a text embedding model to new modalities typically degrades text retrieval quality, and existing omni-modal embedders compensate with multi-billion parameters. We present Omni-Embed-Mini , a 0.9B-parameter model that maps text, speech, audio, images, video, and visually-rich documents into a sin-gle shared cosine space without updating any text-side parameter. Our key insight is that the teacher signal requires no separate embedding model: each media sample is paired with a dense cascaded caption, and the teacher target is simply the frozen backbone’s own embed-ding of that caption. Because teacher and stu-dent share the same backbone weights, they in-habit byte-identical geometry, and lightweight projectors plus phased LoRA adapters on the modality encoders suffice for alignment. Train-ing combines a Matryoshka SigLIP contrastive loss with an online hybrid hard-negative miner whose negatives sharpen as the encoder im-proves. The recipe carries over to a 2.3B vari-ant by swapping in a native vision-language backbone. Omni-Embed-Mini-0.9B keeps its text weights bit-identical to the backbone, so training cannot regress text retrieval (49.57 nDCG@10 on MTEB-v2 BEIR-8), while ex-tending it to five additional modalities, and is ∼2.7 × to 9.5 × smaller than every open omni embedder we compare against. The 2.3B variant is competitive with the closed gemini-embedding-2, edging ahead of it on the overall-modality average. Models, code, data and eval-uation harness are on our project page. 

11 Introduction 

A central goal of recent representation learning is a unified embedding space that binds diverse modal-ities (Girdhar et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2024). In practice, most attempts to jointly contrastive-train text with vision and audio face two coupled fail-ure modes. ( i) Catastrophic forgetting on the text side (McCloskey and Cohen, 1989): fine-tuning a strong text embedder against media inputs shifts the text geometry and degrades MTEB performance. Text is still the most critical modality for real-world search, making this degradation highly undesir-able. ( ii ) Modality bloat : existing omni embed-ders such as LCO-Embedding-Omni-3B/7B (Xiao et al., 2026), BidirLM-Omni-2.5B (Boizard et al., 2026), NVIDIA’s omni-embed-nemotron-3B (Xu et al., 2025), and e5-omni-3B/7B (Chen et al., 2026) have scaled to 2.5B to 8.9B parameters in search of enough capacity to absorb every modal-ity, making them less suited to edge-deployed dynamic retrieval at scale. We term these two coupled failure modes the multimodal expansion dilemma : under current recipes, every additional modality forces a trade-off, because mitigating text-side catastrophic forgetting has typically come with much larger models. We argue that text should not be a co-learner; it should be an immovable anchor . BLIP-2 (Li et al., 2023) bridges a frozen image encoder and a frozen LLM with a small Q-Former, and the first stage of LLaVA (Liu et al., 2023) aligns vision to a frozen LLM through a lightweight projector alone. We translate this principle to retrieval, drawing on the same logic Reimers and Gurevych (2020) used to align a new language to a frozen English teacher’s sentence space: each media sample’s teacher target is the EOS-pooled embedding of a dense cascaded caption, computed by the identical frozen backbone 

that processes the student. Both targets and text queries are therefore produced by byte-identical weights; a modest projector and phased LoRA on the encoders close the modality gap. We call the resulting recipe Omni-Embed-Mini .As a result, Omni-Embed-Mini offers afavourable size-vs-performance trade-off. De-spite being ∼2.7 × to 9.5 × smaller than the open omni-modal embedders listed above, our 0.9Bmodel leaves its foundational text retrieval path untrained and covers five additional modalities in a single sub-billion-parameter model; on me-dia retrieval it trails these larger baselines, as ex-pected at its size. Our 2.3B variant is ahead of the closed gemini-embedding-2 on the overall-modality average ( 51 .39 against 49 .51 ), leading on image, video and visual documents while trailing on text, speech and audio. Our contribution is not a new alignment prim-itive. We combine dense captioning, frozen-backbone self-distillation, parameter-efficient modality adaptation, Matryoshka training and on-line mining into an omni-modal retrieval recipe with one specific invariant: the deployed text in-ference path is unchanged. Concretely: ( i) Zero-regression omni embedding: a 0.9B model (Ta-ble 1) scores 49.57 nDCG@10 on MTEB-v2 BEIR-8 while adding five modalities; the text branch’s weights are bit-identical to the stock 0.6B back-bone, so training cannot regress it. ( ii ) Cascaded-caption self-distillation through a shared back-bone: teacher and student share weights, so cosine alignment in the native pooled hidden space suf-fices: no projection head, no separate embedding teacher, fully cacheable targets. ( iii ) Matryoshka SigLIP + online hybrid hard-negative mining + phased LoRA: a parameter-efficient recipe that carries over to a 2.3B variant by swapping the back-bone for a native vision-language model. ( iv ) To our knowledge, this is the smallest open omni em-bedder at the time of writing, covering text, speech, audio, image, video, and visually-rich documents in one cosine space.

Omni-modal embedders. LCO-Embedding-Omni-3B/7B (Xiao et al., 2026), BidirLM-Omni-2.5B (Boizard et al., 2026), NVIDIA’s omni-embed-nemotron-3B (Xu et al., 2025), and e5-omni-3B/7B (Chen et al., 2026) target a single space across text, image, audio and, apart from BidirLM-Omni, video, but are substantially larger than Omni-Embed-Mini-0.9B. Gemini Embedding 2 (Shanbhogue et al., 2026) is a closed, API-only native multimodal embedder whose parameter count is not disclosed; we report it for reference and exclude it from size-matched comparisons. The closest methodological contrast is e5-omni, which calibrates a LoRA-tuned shared space: modality-aware temperatures, a debiased negative curriculum, and batch whitening with a covariance-alignment loss together reduce mismatches between modalities. We instead anchor to a space that is already calibrated and never updated, so no calibration machinery is needed; our hybrid miner shares only their focus on hard negatives and false-negative suppression. e5-omni-7B leads us on the overall average (52.93 against 51.39), while our 2.3B edges ahead of e5-omni-3B (51.39 against 49.28). We lead on video (55.18 against 32.92 and 44.73) at a half to a quarter of their parameters, and hold a strong text score at 0.9B. Notably, in our text suite e5-omni 

falls from 47.80 to 45.34 from 3B to 7B, which is consistent with the cost that a frozen anchor is designed to avoid. Crucially, absorbing every modality through joint contrastive updating puts the backbone’s native retrieval behaviour at risk, a critical concern for real-world deployment. Among the open omni embedders compared here, ours is the only one that leaves the backbone entirely frozen, text for the 0.9B variant and text plus vision for the 2.3B, adding new modalities through external modality encoders and projectors alone, so as to preserve the backbone’s native capability rather than degrade it (see Fig. 5). 

Anchored vs. contrastive joint alignment. Im-ageBind (Girdhar et al., 2023) uses images as a binding modality: a frozen OpenCLIP ViT-H im-age encoder serves as anchor, and per-modality encoders are trained with symmetric InfoNCE to match the image embedding of naturally co-occurring pairs (image-audio from video, image-depth from RGB-D, etc.). Cross-modal alignment emerges because each modality is independently pulled toward the shared image space. Language-Bind (Zhu et al., 2024) adopts the same anchored-alignment structure but substitutes language for images as the binding modality, freezing a text en-coder and LoRA-adapting per-modality encoders against it. AudioCLIP (Guzhov et al., 2022) takes a different joint-training approach, extending CLIP with a three-way contrastive loss across image, text, and audio. Omni-Embed-Mini shares Lan-guageBind’s choice of text as anchor but departs from both ImageBind and LanguageBind in four ways: ( i) alignment adds asymmetric distillation 

(the frozen backbone embeds a dense caption as the teacher target) to the contrastive objective, so 3each modality is also fitted to a fixed per-sample tar-get vector rather than to batch-level contrast alone; (ii ) pairing data is manufactured via dense caption-ing rather than requiring natural co-occurrence, re-moving ImageBind’s reliance on modality-specific paired corpora; LanguageBind also uses generated captions as contrastive text, whereas we addition-ally use them as a distillation target; ( iii ) the text encoder is not a separate frozen tower but the iden-tical backbone that serves both teacher and student, so both are produced by the same untrained weights and text retrieval cannot regress; ( iv ) because that backbone is a single causal transformer, multiple modalities can be interleaved into one token se-quence and encoded in a single pass, rather than each modality passing through its own tower before a late fusion step. Interleaved multi-modal queries are therefore native to the architecture rather than an added capability; we do not isolate their contri-bution experimentally, and leave a controlled study to future work. Conceptually, this is the cross-modal analogue of Reimers and Gurevych (2020), who aligned a new language to a frozen English teacher’s sentence space via distillation against translated sentences; we substitute “language” with “modality” and “translation” with “dense caption-ing.” Unlike their separately trained student, our text tower is frozen outright. Our recipe builds on several established tech-niques. 3 Method 

3.1 Asymmetric modality alignment 

Two design principles and one training rule gov-ern Omni-Embed-Mini. (P1) Backbone invari-ance. The backbone is never updated: no full fine-tuning, no LoRA on the backbone. This cov-ers the text path for the 0.9B variant, and the text and vision paths for the 2.3B, which is built on a vision-language backbone. Those paths carry no projector and their weights are bit-identical to the stock backbone, so its calibrated retrieval quality is preserved by construction. (P2) Cascaded-caption self-distillation. Each media sample is paired with a dense caption; the embedding of that caption, ob-tained via the same frozen backbone, is the teacher target. Teacher and student share weights, so a cosine alignment loss in the native pooled hidden space suffices. (T1) Alignment-first, then adapta-tion. 3.2 Architecture 

Fig. 4 gives the full data flow for both the student and the teacher path. 

Backbone and pooling. A decoder-only trans-former with causal attention; an EOS-pooling head extracts the last non-padding hidden state and L2-normalises it (no projection head). The 0.9B variant uses Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B (Zhang et al., 2025b) (hidden 1024) with a ViT extracted from Qwen3.5-0.8B (Qwen Team, 2026); the 2.3B variant uses Qwen3-VL-Embedding-2B (Li et al., 2026) (hidden 2048) with native vision. 

Audio path: dual encoder, temporally inter-leaved. Whisper (Radford et al., 2023) (mel) and Dasheng (Dinkel et al., 2024) (raw waveform) run in parallel; each emits 128 projected tokens per 30 s chunk via a 1-D temporal down-projector with SwiGLU residual. The two streams are interleaved in time , (w1, d 1, w 2, d 2, . . . ), yielding 256 tokens per chunk. Rather than a learned cross-attention bottleneck, temporal interleaving is intended as a structural inductive bias that encourages the back-bone’s causal attention to attend jointly to semantic speech content and acoustic texture within local context windows, at zero extra parameter cost. 

Vision path. Custom (0.9B): ViT → 2×2 spa-tial merger → dimension projector → backbone hidden size; for video, a spatial pooler applies cross-attention against a learnable 196-query bud-get so video sequence length is independent of frame count. Native (2.3B): the backbone’s own visual module handles image and video; audio is injected via a forward hook on the embedding layer. Image and video tokens carry multimodal RoPE; audio tokens are spliced as a contiguous temporal span and reuse text RoPE. Media embeddings are spliced into modality-tagged placeholder positions via a one-hot matmul so gradients flow through the projectors and (Phase 2) encoder LoRA. 

3.3 Cascaded-caption self-distillation 

Each training sample i is paired with a dense caption ci generated by Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B-Instruct, with the source dataset’s ground-truth cap-tion pinned in the system prompt as a grounding prior (§B). The teacher embedding ti is the L2-normalised EOS-pooled output of the frozen back-bone applied to ci; because the backbone is frozen, 

ti is constant across training and is computed once and cached on disk under a stable sample id. The student embedding si is the same EOS-pool ap-plied to the token sequence with media spliced in. Because si and ti share the backbone and pool-5ing, they live in identical geometry; no auxiliary projection head is required.

3.5 Online hybrid hard-negative mining 

Hard negatives are supplied by a background subsystem maintaining FAISS-CPU (Johnson et al., 2019) inner-product indices (one for text, one per media modality), in the spirit of ANCE (Xiong et al., 2020). A lightweight text 

cycle ( ∼10 ×/epoch) re-embeds captions via the text-only path and retains the top-k neighbours per query. A more compute-intensive media cy-cle ( ∼5×/epoch) re-embeds samples per modality through the full model into per-modality indices. The miner shares the live model with the train-ing loop, so each cycle returns negatives from the 

current representation: as the encoder improves, mined negatives become harder. Crucially, because the text branch never receives gradient, the text-side index is perfectly stable; only the media-side index drifts, and it is refreshed so as to supply harder negatives as the encoder improves. This sta-bility is intended to guard against modality collapse 

without ever destabilising text geometry. Con-currency, cache semantics, and failure handling are detailed in §D. 

4 Training Data: Dense Caption Grounding 

A central claim of this paper is that the success of frozen-backbone distillation depends critically on the density of the captioning signal. We therefore treat data preparation as a first-class methodologi-cal contribution rather than a logistical detail. 

Source corpus. We train on the caption slice of our omni-modal caption corpus. From 590,858 total rows, filtering to caption-category rows across the speech, audio, image, video, and visual-doc configurations yields 242,080 single-turn samples; a subsequent duration filter (removing audio/video clips outside the supported length range) reduces this to 227,704 effective training rows (Fig. 2); caption length varies sharply by modality (Fig. 3). The omni split (cross-modal multi-turn chat) and chat samples are excluded; their narrow answers are unsuitable distillation targets. 

Cascaded caption pipeline. Every row is the output of a single low-temperature ( T =0 .7) call to Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B-Instruct, with the source dataset’s ground-truth caption interpolated as a “Reference caption” inside a modality-specific sys-tem prompt. This grounds the cascaded cap-tion against the source, which may reduce un-constrained hallucination but does not preclude it. Visual-doc rows use an “expert document an-alyst” prompt with no reference caption (none exists). Full prompts and worked examples are in Appendix §B. Dense captions are the single most important data-side ingredient of the recipe (as we demon-strate in §6.2): they expose more facets of each sample (objects, attributes, layout, temporal events, acoustic environment, OCR text) than short crowd-sourced captions, which provide a weaker, bag-of-words-like signal. We ablate this in Table 2, and worked qualitative examples per modality are in Appendix Table 10. Because every teacher target is machine-generated, caption noise is a direct risk to the learned geometry. Four properties of the recipe 6limit how far it can propagate: a grounded caption-ing prompt, a pooled-embedding target rather than token-level supervision, a 0.92 cosine cutoff in min-ing, and a frozen text path that no captioner bias can reach. These contain noise rather than verify its absence; we do not filter captions for factuality. §E gives the full argument and its limits.

Targeted ablations isolate four design choices: frozen backbone, dense captions, hybrid mining, and Matryoshka propagation.

6.2 Dense vs. original captions 

Table 2 shows the smallest gap is on speech (+2.68), where source transcripts already carry most of the content; the largest is on video (+14.06), where short clip captions miss nearly every temporal event. The same effect motivated the dense re-captioning pipeline of Li et al. (2024) for VLM pre-training; it is equally decisive for retrieval. 

6.3 Hybrid mining 

Fig. 6 shows that only combined mining separates clearly from no mining ( +2 .6 on the mean over the five media modalities); the gaps among the other three configurations are comparable to the seed spread reported below. Text-only mining alone slightly hurts on several modalities (the student sees harder text negatives but no harder media ones, biasing the projector); media-only gives a small improvement on most modalities; combin-ing both yields the best overall score, though per-modality rankings swap on video and on visual documents, where media-only falls marginally be-low no-mining. Visual-document retrieval is in fact the modality least sensitive to mining: the four configurations agree to within 0.6 nDCG@10 on ViDoRe-V3 (46.42 to 46.98). What moves that modality is caption quality rather than neg-ative sampling, as replacing the dense cascaded captions with the datasets’ original captions costs 4.8 nDCG@10 (46.98 to 42.21). The two miners contribute complementary pressure: text-mining sharpens the caption-text geometry the teacher signal targets, while media-mining sharpens the modality-specific student geometry; dropping ei-ther leaves a slack axis along which the projector can drift. The main recipe uses both miners. 

Seed sensitivity. Across three random seeds (42, 71 and 1234) of the 10-epoch no-mining con-figuration, the model is stable across all five trained modalities: image scores 27 .48 ± 0.43 ,general audio 33 .96 ± 0.59 , video 19 .82 ± 0.95 ,speech 43 .10 ± 1.03 , and visual-document retrieval 

45 .48 ± 1.76 (mean ± standard deviation over the three seeds). Text is identical across all three at 49.57: the text branch carries no trainable param-eters, so every 0.9B checkpoint returns the same value. Per-seed numbers are in §F. 

6.4 Matryoshka behaviour 

Because MRL slicing acts on the backbone’s pooled hidden state with no learned head, Ma-tryoshka behaviour at every dimension is inherited from the backbone (Kusupati et al., 2022; OpenAI, 2024). Averaged-probe AVG falls only slightly from 1024 to 128 for both variants, whereas the omni-specific baselines fall away far more steeply (Fig. 8). For the 0.9B, a 128-dim truncation reduces on-disk footprint 8 ×: a 10M-item omni index in float32 fits in ∼5 GB rather than ∼41 GB, at mod-est cost in quality. Further analyses are in §F. 

7 Conclusion 

We introduced Omni-Embed-Mini , a 0.9B omni-modal retrieval model that binds text, speech, au-dio, images, video, and visually-rich documents into a unified space by recasting cross-modal align-ment as self-distillation through a shared, frozen 

text backbone. By keeping the text encoder fixed and aligning every other modality to its represen-tation, we sidestep the catastrophic forgetting that typically plagues multi-modal fine-tuning and turn omni-modal training into a lightweight projector-plus-LoRA exercise. The model preserves its back-bone’s text retrieval quality while remaining com-petitive with open omni embedders ∼2.7 × to 9.5 ×

larger, and carries over to a 2.3B variant competi-9tive with baselines 2 × to 4 × its size. Because the backbone is frozen, future text-embedder improve-ments can in principle be adopted by re-running only the projector and LoRA training, as our back-bone swap from the 0.6B text encoder to Qwen3-VL-Embedding-2B illustrates. Open threads in-clude learned mixing of the dual audio-encoder tokens, a Mixture-of-Encoders variant that routes inputs to specialist branches, and replacing per-frame mean-pooling for video (§I) with a native spatial pooler that better preserves temporal and spatial structure. Beyond architecture, the most pressing community gap is evaluation: existing benchmarks each probe a single modality slice, and none evaluate cross-modal retrieval in arbitrary query →corpus combinations across all six modali-ties simultaneously. 

Limitations 

Omni-Embed-Mini is a research prototype for omni-modal retrieval and is not validated for high-stakes deployment. Although the model provides efficient alignment across text, speech, audio, im-age, video, and visually-rich documents, it may still retrieve incorrect, biased, private, or misleading items when used in downstream systems. There-fore, it should not be used as a standalone decision system in sensitive domains without task-specific validation and human oversight. The model’s shared embedding space is con-strained by the frozen text backbone used to pre-serve text-side retrieval performance. As a result, modality-specific phenomena that are difficult to verbalise, such as complex acoustic textures, ab-stract visual patterns, or fine-grained temporal cues, may not be fully represented. The cascaded-caption self-distillation pipeline also depends on teacher-generated captions, which may contain hallucina-tions, omissions, or biases that are inherited by the student model. We do not filter captions for factual-ity, and we do not verify them against their source media; the only automatic check in the pipeline is a 0.92 cosine threshold used to discard likely false negatives during mining. We also did not run a controlled caption-perturbation study, so we can-not quantify how far retrieval quality degrades as caption quality falls. What we can report is that caption quality matters a great deal in aggregate: replacing our dense cascaded captions with the datasets’ original captions costs 7.01 points on the mean of speech, audio, image and video (Table 2) and 4.8 nDCG@10 on visual documents. Because a single captioner produces every target, any sys-tematic bias it carries is applied uniformly across the corpus rather than averaged out, which is the failure mode we would most want to measure. Plau-sible mitigations we have not implemented include cross-checking captions against a second captioner, filtering on caption-to-media agreement scored by an independent model, and sampling captions for human verification; we regard a caption-robustness study along these lines as the most useful next step for this recipe. The audio pathway currently uses a fixed tempo-ral interleaving of speech and environmental-audio tokens, which may be suboptimal for inputs dom-inated by only one audio type. In addition, the compact 0.9B and 2.3B model scales are optimised for efficiency, but may limit performance on com-plex reasoning-heavy multimodal retrieval tasks. Finally, the evaluation is limited by the coverage of available benchmarks. Existing datasets do not yet provide a unified any-to-any retrieval bench-mark across all six supported modalities, so our results may not generalise to all query-corpus com-binations, languages, domains, data qualities, or real-world retrieval workflows. 

Ethical Considerations 

This work is intended for research and educational use in omni-modal representation learning and re-trieval. Omni-Embed-Mini should not be used for autonomous decision-making in high-stakes do-mains such as medicine, law, finance, hiring, edu-cation, security, or public-sector services without appropriate validation and human supervision. The main ethical risks include inaccurate re-trieval, bias amplification, privacy leakage, and misuse. Since the model aligns multiple modal-ities into a shared embedding space, errors or biases from training data, teacher-generated cap-tions, and pre-trained encoders may affect retrieval quality across languages, accents, visual appear-ances, environments, document types, and cul-tural contexts. Downstream users should conduct safety, fairness, privacy, robustness, and human-in-the-loop evaluations before applying the model in sensitive settings. We also disclose our use of generative AI tools. Writing-support models were used for grammar, style, and clarity improvements. All technical claims, experimental results, analyses, and con-10 clusions were authored, checked, and verified by the authors; any coding assistance was limited to boilerplate scaffolding, minor refactoring, or for-matting support.

D Mining subsystem 

The miner runs as a single background thread that shares the live training model with the main loop, avoiding the roughly 16 GB extra HBM cost of a separate model copy. Within-cycle weight drift between miner and main thread is empirically neg-ligible because the LoRA delta per optimiser step is small and the embedding geometry is dominated by the frozen backbone. 

Cadence. Every modality receives mined hard negatives. The miner alternates between two cy-cle types, gated on the training step counter. A

text cycle re-embeds the captions of all five media modalities, builds a CPU FAISS index, and writes hard-negative ids into the negative cache; it fires roughly ten times per epoch and finishes in about thirty seconds. A media cycle re-embeds the sam-ples of each media modality (speech, audio, image, video and visual document) through the full model into per-modality indices; it fires roughly five times per epoch and takes 12 to 20 minutes. Triggers are coalesced, so multiple firings during a long cycle collapse to a single follow-up. 

Concurrency and cache semantics. A single forward-lock serialises miner and main-thread for-ward passes at batch granularity: the main thread holds the lock for the entire forward, backward and optimiser step, and the miner acquires it per encode-batch, yielding fine-grained alternation rather than overlap. The negative cache is keyed by sample id and overwrites on write, so media-mined en-tries supersede earlier text-mined ones for the same sample. If the cache is empty for a given sample at batch time the collator falls back to in-batch neg-atives plus distillation, recovering the no-mining ablation behaviour with no special-case code. 

E Caption grounding and noise safeguards 

Because every teacher target is machine-generated, caption noise is a direct risk to the learned geom-etry. Four properties of the recipe limit how far that noise can propagate. First, the captioner is 

grounded : the source dataset’s own caption is in-terpolated into the prompt, so the model elaborates on a human-written anchor rather than describing the media unconstrained. Second, the target is a 

pooled embedding , not the caption tokens; the stu-dent fits a single vector, so local errors in wording are attenuated by pooling rather than supervised token by token. Compression is not correction, however: systematic hallucinations, omissions and semantic biases can still propagate into the student through the pooled target. Third, hard-negative mining applies a 0.92 cosine cutoff, which discards candidates too close to the anchor to be genuine negatives and so removes the mining pairs most likely to be caption artefacts. Fourth, the text path is frozen, so no amount of captioner bias can reach text retrieval: whatever the captions contain, the text branch is the stock backbone. Visual documents are the informative case: they are the one modality captioned with no reference caption to ground against, and they still reach strong retrieval, which shows that fully generated captions can provide effective supervision in this setting. It does not measure robustness to halluci-nated or systematically biased captions. Cross-modal alignment probe. During training we monitor a SciFact text-vs-speech NDCG@10 convergence probe every 10% of an epoch. The synthesised speech version of each claim is en-coded through the full multimodal path (Whisper + Dasheng → projectors → backbone) while the corpus remains text. Media-to-media alignment. Routing every modality through the same shared backbone and aligning each one to the caption text places all media in one geometry after training. We never train a media-to-media objective, but because every modality shares the text axis as a common reference frame, pairings such as image ↔audio, audio ↔video and image ↔document can in 24 principle be compared directly. This mirrors the emergent alignment reported by Image-Bind (Girdhar et al., 2023), here induced by a text anchor rather than by contrasting modalities pair-wise, with the frozen text-embedding backbone supplying the shared metric. 

Single-stage training. LLaVA (Liu et al., 2023) and most VL models train in two distinct stages with two distinct datasets. Omni-Embed-Mini runs as a single-stage pipeline: the dataset is loaded once and the only “phasing” is internal to the opti-misation schedule (projectors only, then projectors plus encoder LoRA; §3.1). The two phases share the same data-loader, the same dense cascaded cap-tions, and the same loss, with no hand-off. This is possible because dense cascaded captions carry both alignment signal and task signal in one row.

H Hyperparameters and training schedule 

Table 14 lists the hyperparameters of the smallest variant, Omni-Embed-Mini-0.9B. Training runs on 8 GPUs with DDP, BF16 mixed precision, AdamW, and a single cosine schedule with warmup. Batch size is held near-uniform across modalities (image, visual document, audio and speech at 32; video at 8 due to its patch-token footprint). Every number we report for a given variant comes from a single checkpoint of that variant, evaluated across all six modalities. 

I Additional Limitations 

• Vision modes diverge between the 0.9B and 2.3B variants. The 0.9B variant uses a ViT extracted from the vision-native Qwen3.5-0.8B model because the 0.6B text backbone has no vision module; the 2.3B variant reuses its backbone’s native vision module. The train-ing configuration accommodates both, but cross-variant ablations of the two vision strategies are left to future work. • Visual-document captions are roughly six times longer than other modalities. Visual-document dense captions average about 4,157 characters versus about 582 for audio. The frozen backbone’s 2,048-token context truncates the long tail of visual-document captions; trunca-tion is consistent between teacher and student paths so cosine alignment is unaffected, but the upper end of visual-doc detail is lost from the contrastive signal. • Video uses per-frame mean-pooled embed-dings on MMEB-V2. For all omni-style models, the benchmark’s own 8 frames per video are embedded as images and mean-pooled. This keeps results directly comparable but caps video performance below what a native temporal encoder could reach.
