Abstract 

Vision-language models (VLMs) have achieved strong performance in multimodal reasoning, yet they remain prone to generating plausible but incorrect answers. Self-verification offers a practical way to improve answer reliability without relying on external judges, but existing methods typically depend on a single verification criterion or fixed prompt, resulting in incomplete and unstable reliability estimates. We first systematically analyze how verifier capability and prompt design affect verification performance. Our findings show that stronger verifiers provide more reliable judgments, while verification performance is highly sensitive to prompt choice, with no single prompt consistently dominating across tasks. Guided by these findings, we propose MOTIVE , a Multi-View Self-Verificati On wi Th Rel Iability-Guided Selecti VE Rethinking framework for reliable multimodal reasoning. MOTIVE evaluates each candidate answer from complementary verification perspectives and learns a correctness-aligned reliability score through correctness-grounded multi-view verification learning. During inference, this score governs an accept-or-rethink decision, allowing reliable answers to be returned directly while uncertain ones trigger history-guided rethinking. Extensive experiments across diverse multimodal benchmarks and VLM backbones demonstrate that MOTIVE consistently outperforms strong self-verification and self-correction baselines. Further results show that reliable verification improves accept-or-rethink decisions and reduces unnecessary reasoning turns, enabling more reliable and efficient self-verification without an external judge. https://github.com/misswayguy/Motive 

1 Introduction 

Recent advances in vision-language models (VLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in multimodal understanding, enabling strong performance across diverse visual question answering and reasoning tasks (Dai et al., 2023; Dong and Crisan, 2025; Bordes et al., 2024). However, despite their impressive capabilities, VLMs remain prone to generating plausible but incorrect answers due to visual ambiguity and reasoning errors (Huang et al., 2025; Hou et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024b). Therefore, enabling VLMs to assess the reliability of their own predictions is crucial for building more trustworthy multimodal systems (Ghosh et al., 2024). To improve the reliability of VLMs, recent studies have explored self-verification mechanisms that enable models to examine and assess their own generated answers (Zhang et al., 2025b; Chen et al., 2024a; Cheng et al., 2024). Compared with external verification approaches that require additional models or computational 1arXiv:2610.07018v1 [cs.AI] 4 Oct 2026 MOTIVE When to Rethink 

resources, self-verification provides a more efficient way to evaluate predictions within the same multimodal model (Campbell et al., 2024; Anis et al., 2025). However, existing self-verification methods typically rely on a single verification criterion or a fixed checking procedure (Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023), which may fail to capture diverse types of errors in multimodal reasoning. As a result, how to obtain more reliable and comprehensive verification signals remains an open challenge. To better understand the factors affecting self-verification reliability, we conduct preliminary anal-yses on verification capability and prompt design in Section 2. Our studies reveal two important observations. ❶ Verification effectiveness depends on verifier capability. Stronger verifiers provide more reliable assessments by better understanding visual evidence, question semantics, and answer consis-tency, indicating that verification quality is closely related to the reasoning ability of the verifier. ❷

Verification performance is sensitive to verification prompt design. Different verification prompts exhibit complementary strengths, while no single prompt consistently performs best across different multimodal tasks. These findings suggest that reliable self-verification benefits from diverse verification perspectives and the effective aggregation of complementary signals. Guided by these findings, we introduce MOTIVE (Multi-View Self-Verificati On wi Th Rel Iability-Guided Selecti VE Rethinking), a self-verification framework for reliable multimodal reasoning. MOTIVE evaluates each candidate answer through complementary verification views and aggregates their outputs into a unified reliability score. Correctness-grounded verification learning supervises both view-specific judgments and the aggregated score using automatically derived candidate-correctness labels. During inference, the learned score governs an accept-or-rethink decision: reliable answers are returned directly, whereas uncertain ones trigger history-guided rethinking. This design improves verification decisions within the same backbone VLM without requiring an external judge. Our efforts are unfolded with the following four thrusts: 

⋆ (Preliminary Analysis) We systematically investigate verification capability and prompt design, showing that stronger verifiers improve answer reliability, while self-verification remains highly prompt-sensitive and no single prompt consistently dominates across tasks. 

⋆ (Algorithm) Guided by these findings, we propose MOTIVE , a multi-view self-verification framework that uses correctness-grounded verification supervision to support reliability-guided accept-or-rethink decisions without an external judge. 

⋆ (Experiments) Extensive experiments on five benchmarks and three VLM backbones demonstrate that 

MOTIVE consistently outperforms strong self-verification and self-correction baselines. 

⋆ (Extra Findings) We further find that reliable verification enables earlier stopping with fewer unnecessary rethinking turns, while the best individual verification view varies across datasets and backbones, confirming their task-dependent complementarity.

① Verification Enhances Final Answer Reliability. As shown in Figure 1, introducing verification consis-tently improves final-answer performance over direct answering across different backbones and benchmarks. This indicates that the initial responses of VLMs are not always sufficiently reliable. By determining whether an answer should be accepted or regenerated, verification provides an opportunity to correct unreliable responses without modifying or fine-tuning the backbone model. 

② Stronger Judges Improve Verification Effectiveness. We further observe that larger InternVL3 judges generally achieve better final performance across different Qwen backbones and multimodal benchmarks. This indicates that verification effectiveness depends on the judge’s multimodal reasoning capability, particularly its ability to assess the consistency among visual evidence, question semantics, and the generated answer. External verification improves VLM reliability but requires an auxiliary judge and introduces additional computational cost, motivating self-verification within the backbone VLM. This provides a more practical way to assess answer reliability without requiring an additional model during use. 

2.2 Verification Prompt Design 

We next examine how prompt design affects self-verification performance. Since external judges introduce additional computational cost, we use the same backbone VLM for both answer generation and verification. Based on representative self-correction and self-verification protocols (He et al., 2025; Weng et al., 2023; Dhuliawala et al., 2024), we compare two prompt families: five predefined Direct Verification Prompts (DVP-1–DVP-5), provided in Appendix B.1, which assess the initial answer from complementary perspectives, and Generated Verification Questions (GVQ-1–GVQ-3), which generate and answer one, two, or three auxiliary verification questions, respectively, before producing a final Y ES /N O verdict. A Y ES verdict retains the initial answer, whereas N O triggers rethinking and regeneration. We conduct the analysis using Qwen2.5-VL-3B on VQA, ScienceQA, and TableVQA, varying only the verification prompt. The main observations are summarized below. 

① Self-Verification Is Highly Prompt-Sensitive. As shown in Table 1, changing only the verification prompt leads to clear performance variations under the same backbone, datasets, and verification protocol. This indicates that self-verification cannot be reliably elicited by an arbitrary checking instruction. Its effectiveness depends on whether the prompt provides a clear criterion for assessing the initial answer, as different prompts may lead the same VLM to produce inconsistent verification decisions across multimodal tasks.

② No Single Direct Prompt Consistently Dominates. Among DVP-1 to DVP-5, the best-performing prompt varies across benchmarks, showing that no single direct prompt provides a universally reliable verification view. Although all DVP prompts follow the same direct Y ES /N O verification format, their effects are task-dependent, suggesting that each prompt captures only part of the evidence needed for reliable answer checking. Relying on one fixed prompt may therefore overfit the verification behavior to a specific benchmark or question type. This motivates the use of complementary direct prompts to obtain more reliable and informative verification signals across tasks. 

③ Generated Verification Questions Introduce Instability. GVQ-style prompts are generally less stable than direct verification prompts, and their performance further decreases as more auxiliary questions are generated. Although GVQ makes the verification process more explicit, these intermediate questions are generated by the same VLM that produced the initial answer. As a result, they may inherit the model’s original uncertainty, shift attention away from the original query, or introduce additional reasoning errors before the final verdict. This suggests that increasing the number of self-generated verification steps does not necessarily strengthen verification, but can amplify intermediate uncertainty and error propagation. These findings indicate that the central challenge of self-verification is not adding more reasoning steps, but establishing a reliable criterion for judging whether the initial answer should be trusted. A single direct prompt provides incomplete and prompt-sensitive signals, while generated verification questions introduce uncertainty. This motivates a multi-view self-verification framework that aggregates complementary signals and learns to distinguish reliable answers from unreliable ones. 

3 Method 

We propose MOTIVE , a calibrated verification framework for reliable multimodal reasoning, as shown in Figure 2. Given an image–question pair (I, q ), a multimodal policy first generates a candidate answer and then evaluates its reliability through multiple verification views. Instead of treating self-verification as a post-hoc prompting heuristic, MOTIVE learns a correctness-aligned reliability signal that determines whether the model should accept the current answer or allocate additional computation to rethink. The framework jointly optimizes answer generation, correctness-grounded multi-view verification, and reliability-guided selective rethinking.

4 Experiments 

Datasets. We conduct experiments on five visual question answering benchmarks: ReFocus (Fu et al., 2025), VQA (Antol et al., 2015), SLaKE (Liu et al., 2021), ScienceQA (Lu et al., 2022), and TableVQA (Kim et al., 2024). The selected benchmarks cover general image understanding, structured-image reasoning, medical VQA, scientific multimodal reasoning, and table-based visual question answering. Specifically, VQA evaluates general visual question answering over natural images; ReFocus focuses on structured visual inputs such as charts and tables; SLaKE targets medical VQA with radiology images; ScienceQA requires image-grounded scientific reasoning; and TableVQA tests fine-grained understanding of table images. We follow the official splits and evaluation protocols for all datasets. More dataset details are provided in Appendix A.1. 

Models and Baselines. We evaluate MOTIVE on three representative publicly available VLM backbones: Qwen2.5-VL-3B, Qwen2.5-VL-7B (Bai et al., 2025a), and InternVL3-2B (Zhu et al., 2025). These models cover different parameter scales and model families, allowing us to evaluate whether MOTIVE consistently improves verification reliability across diverse vision-language backbones rather than being specific to a particular model architecture. We compare MOTIVE with several groups of baselines. The first baseline is the vanilla VLM, which directly generates the final answer without self-verification. The second group includes prompt-level self-reasoning and self-verification methods, including Self-Consistency (Wang et al., 2023), Self-Verification (Weng et al., 2023), and Chain-of-Verification (Dhuliawala et al., 2024). The third group includes recent multimodal self-improvement and self-correction methods, including ADPO (Qiu et al., 2026), R 3V (Cheng et al., 2025), and LLaVA-SCo (Liu et al., 2025). For methods originally developed for language-only reasoning, we adapt their verification or correction prompts to the vision-language setting by conditioning on the same image-question input. All baselines are evaluated under the same datasets, answer-matching protocol, and decoding budget unless otherwise specified.

Implementation Details. For each backbone, all methods are evaluated using the same dataset splits and answer-matching protocol. We train MOTIVE with the AdamW optimizer using an initial learning rate of 

1 × 10 −6 and BF16 precision. During GRPO training, we use a rollout group size of G = 8 and a sampling temperature of 1.0. We employ K = 5 verification views and aggregate their reliability scores by arithmetic averaging. The acceptance threshold is set to τ = 0 .5, the maximum number of candidate-answer turns is 

T = 3 , and the verification objective is weighted by λver = 0 .1. We use official evaluation scripts when available; otherwise, multiple-choice and open-form answers are evaluated by exact option matching and normalized answer matching, respectively. Additional hyperparameters are provided in Appendix A.2.

We compare conventional Self-Verification with MOTIVE across five benchmarks using three VLM backbones. As shown in Table 3, MOTIVE consistently achieves higher SVA across all evaluated backbones and datasets, with particularly clear improvements on tasks requiring fine-grained visual grounding and structured evidence checking. 4.3 Effectiveness of Multi-View Verification 

To verify that the gains of MOTIVE arise from combining complementary verification views rather than from a single strong prompt, we evaluate five single-view variants and the full model on five benchmarks with three VLM backbones. Each variant retains one verification view while all other settings remain unchanged. As shown in Table 4, the best single view changes across datasets and backbones, and no individual view is consistently superior. In contrast, MOTIVE achieves the highest and most stable overall accuracy, validating the necessity of multi-view verification. 

4.4 Efficient Reasoning via Reliable Early Stopping 

To examine whether MOTIVE can avoid unnecessary rethinking, we compare its average reasoning turns with conventional Self-Verification under the same maximum budget of T = 3 . A lower value indicates that the model can accept reliable answers earlier and requires fewer repeated revisions. As shown in Table 5, 

MOTIVE consistently reduces the average number of reasoning turns across all backbones and benchmarks. The reduction is more pronounced on challenging tasks such as VQA and SLaKE, where conventional Self-Verification is more likely to repeatedly reconsider uncertain answers, while smaller reductions are observed on ScienceQA, where reliable answers can already be identified relatively early. These results demonstrate that MOTIVE not only improves answer reliability but also enables more efficient reasoning through better early-stopping decisions.

We investigate the effect of different aggregation strategies for combining multiple verification views while keeping all other training and inference settings unchanged. Specifically, we compare Majority Vote, which first converts each verification view into a binary decision and then selects the majority prediction, with Averaging, which directly aggregates the continuous YES probabilities produced by all verification views. As shown in Table 7, Averaging consistently achieves better performance than Majority Vote across different backbones and benchmarks. This indicates that preserving the confidence information contained in each verification view provides a more reliable estimation of answer correctness than using only discrete verification decisions.

5 Related Work 

Self-Verification in Multimodal Reasoning. Recent advances in multimodal reasoning have increasingly explored self-verification and self-correction mechanisms to improve the reliability of vision-language models (Ding et al., 2026). Rather than relying solely on a single-pass prediction, these methods encourage

models to re-examine their intermediate reasoning or final answers through reflection (Yan et al., 2024; Cao et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025a), critique (Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2025b), verifier-guided assessment (Weng et al., 2023; Ding and Zhang, 2025; Wu et al., 2025a), or iterative correct (Gou et al., 2024; Manakul et al., 2023; Cheng et al., 2025; He et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025). Such designs have shown promise in reducing inconsistent predictions and improving performance on complex visual reasoning tasks (Qian et al., 2026; Lee et al., 2024), where errors often arise from weak visual grounding or unstable multi-step inference (Yin et al., 2024; Si et al., 2021). However, most existing approaches treat verification primarily as an inference-time strategy for refining final outputs (Luo et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026a), instead of explicitly modeling the supervision carried by the verification process itself. As a result, the fine-grained signals embedded in multi-turn self-verification, especially those associated with intermediate candidate answers, remain underexploited during training. 

Verifiable Supervision and Training Signals for VLMs. Beyond inference-time correction, another line of research seeks to improve reasoning models by turning verifiable outcomes into explicit training signals (Cobbe et al., 2021; Lightman et al., 2023; Shao et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2026b,a). In tasks where answer correctness can be automatically checked, prior work has explored reward-based learning (Bai et al., 2025b; Meng et al., 2025), process supervision (Zhang et al., 2025a; Li et al., 2026b; Han et al., 2026), and intermediate guidance to enhance model reliability without relying entirely on manual annotations (Guo et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b; Zhan et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2025). These methods demonstrate that verifiable feedback can serve as an effective and scalable supervision source for vision-language models, especially in settings that require structured reasoning and precise decision making (Chen and Song, 2025; Marsili and Gkioxari, 2025; Zhou et al., 2025). Nevertheless, existing approaches typically emphasize final-answer correctness (Wang et al., 2025b; Ong et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024), while paying less attention to the supervision value of intermediate verification trajectories. In particular, they seldom model the consistency between round-wise candidate answers and corresponding verification judgments, leaving multi-turn self-verification signals insufficiently exploited during training. 

6 Conclusion 

In this work, we investigated self-verification in vision-language models and showed that its effectiveness depends on verifier capability and prompt design, with no single verification prompt consistently dominating across tasks. Guided by these findings, we proposed MOTIVE , a multi-view self-verification framework that aggregates complementary verification signals and learns correctness-aligned reliability through view-level and aggregate-level verification supervision. The resulting reliability score supports accept-or-rethink decisions, enabling uncertain answers to be selectively revised within the same backbone VLM without requiring an external judge. Experiments across five benchmarks and three VLM backbones demonstrate that 

MOTIVE improves final-answer performance, verification decision quality, and early stopping.

A Experiment Settings 

A.1 Additional Dataset Details ReFocus. ReFocus (Fu et al., 2025) evaluates structured-image understanding over visually complex tables and charts. The benchmark contains 2,219 question–answer pairs collected from six subsets covering Wikipedia tables, synthetically rendered tables, table verification, scientific charts, and horizontal and vertical bar charts. Its questions require fine-grained information extraction, comparison, counting, aggregation, and multi-step reasoning over structured visual content. 

VQA. VQA (Antol et al., 2015) is a large-scale open-ended visual question answering benchmark based primarily on natural images from Microsoft COCO. Its real-image portion contains approximately 204,000 images and 614,000 human-written questions covering object recognition, attributes, counting, spatial relationships, activities, and commonsense reasoning. Multiple human answers are collected for each question to account for linguistic variation and answer ambiguity. 

SLaKE. SLaKE (Liu et al., 2021) is a bilingual medical VQA dataset containing 642 radiological images and 14,028 English–Chinese question–answer pairs. It includes CT, MRI, and X-ray images covering the head, neck, chest, abdomen, and pelvic cavity. The questions examine imaging modality, anatomical structures, organ positions, abnormalities, and medical knowledge. We use the English-language questions in our experiments. 

ScienceQA. ScienceQA (Lu et al., 2022) contains 21,208 multiple-choice questions collected from elementary- and high-school science curricula, of which 10,332 are associated with image contexts. The questions span natural science, social science, and language science and may include scientific diagrams, natural images, textual contexts, candidate answers, lectures, and explanations. We use the image-based subset to evaluate multimodal scientific reasoning. 

TableVQA. TableVQA-Bench (Kim et al., 2024) contains 1,500 question–answer pairs constructed from table images across multiple domains, including Wikipedia and financial documents. The tables exhibit diverse layouts, styles, fonts, borders, and cell structures. The benchmark evaluates visual cell recognition, row–column alignment, comparison, aggregation, numerical calculation, and reasoning over structured table content. 

A.2 Additional Implementation Details 

All experiments are conducted on NVIDIA B200 GPUs. 1 The internal weights of the verification objective are set to βagg = 1 .0 and βcal = 0 .1, while the computation-aware reward coefficient is set to c = 0 .05 . The verification loss weight is set to λver = 0 .1. During inference, MOTIVE uses K = 5 verification views, an acceptance threshold of τ = 0 .5, and a maximum of T = 3 reasoning turns. For each verification view, the view-specific reliability estimate is given by the probability assigned to the Y ES output, and the final reliability score is obtained by averaging the estimates across all verification views. The computation-aware rollout reward is used to compute group-relative advantages in GRPO, while the auxiliary verification objective jointly supervises view-specific judgments and aggregated reliability scores.

B Prompt Details 

B.1 Direct Verification Prompt Templates 

In Section 2.2, we analyze different verification prompt designs and observe that no single direct verification prompt consistently dominates across all benchmarks. Based on this analysis, we adopt the five predefined Direct Verification Prompts (DVPs) as complementary verification views in MOTIVE , as described in Sec-tion 3. These prompts evaluate a candidate answer from different perspectives, covering overall correctness, visual evidence support, backward evidence checking, global contextual consistency, and error discovery. Specifically, DVP-1 performs general correctness verification without additional constraints; DVP-2 focuses on whether the answer is directly grounded in observable image evidence; DVP-3 verifies whether the evidence required by the answer can be identified by reasoning back from the predicted conclusion; DVP-4 examines the consistency between the answer, the question context, and the overall visual scene; and DVP-5 adopts an error-seeking perspective to detect potential contradictions or mistakes in the candidate answer. Together, these complementary views provide diverse criteria for evaluating answer reliability. The complete DVP templates are summarized in Figure 7.

C Additional Experiments 

C.1 Sensitivity Analysis of Acceptance Threshold 

We further investigate the effect of the acceptance threshold τ used in the accept-or-rethink decision. While a lower threshold allows the model to terminate verification more easily, it may accept unreliable answers, whereas a higher threshold encourages additional verification but may introduce unnecessary rethinking. As shown in Figure 10, the performance remains relatively stable across different threshold values, indicating that MOTIVE is not sensitive to a specific manually selected threshold. The default setting of τ = 0 .5 achieves the best performance across all five benchmarks, providing an effective balance between reliable answer acceptance and selective rethinking. These results demonstrate that the learned reliability score provides a reliable decision signal for controlling the verification process.      

> Figure 10: Effect of the acceptance threshold τ.Accuracy (%) on five benchmarks under different values of τusing Qwen2.5-VL-3B.

C.2 Computation-Aware Reward Analysis 

We further analyze the effect of the computation-aware reward coefficient c by varying its value while keeping all other training configurations unchanged. The coefficient c penalizes unnecessary rethinking turns, encouraging the model to terminate verification once a reliable answer is obtained. As shown in Fig. 11, increasing c consistently reduces the number of rethinking turns across different benchmarks, while the final accuracy remains largely stable. Although larger penalties achieve stronger computation reduction, an excessive value ( c = 0 .10 ) may cause premature stopping and slightly degrade accuracy. The selected coefficient c = 0 .05 provides a favorable trade-off between maintaining answer quality and avoiding redundant rethinking. 

C.3 Maximum Reasoning Turns Analysis 

We further analyze the effect of the maximum reasoning budget T while keeping all other settings unchanged. As shown in Fig. 12, increasing the maximum number of reasoning turns from T = 2 to T = 3 consistently improves the performance across VQA, ScienceQA, and TableVQA, showing that one additional rethinking opportunity is beneficial for correcting uncertain answers. In contrast, further increasing the budget to 

T = 4 or T = 5 only yields marginal gains, indicating diminishing returns from additional reasoning turns. Therefore, we adopt T = 3 as the default setting, since it captures most of the performance benefit while avoiding unnecessary extra computation.
