## ABSTRACT 

Multimodal 3D Human Pose Estimation (3D HPE) combines complementary information from RGB, LiDAR, and mmWave radar, but models trained on correlated observations from the same individuals, raise privacy risks overlooked by record level analysis. We present a unified framework for multimodal 3D HPE that couples kinematics-induced sensor fusion with subject level privacy auditing and private training. First, our multimodal model aligns modality specific joint representation, injects skeletal structure and adaptively aggregates complementary sensor evidence for accurate pose prediction. Second, we formulate a black-box subject membership inference attack for 3D HPE, complemented by an empirical pointwise maximal leakage analysis, which characterizes how individual attack score outcomes change inference about the membership outcome. Third, we instantiate user-level differential privacy via Action Temporal Stratification, a population weighted within-subject sampling strategy that enforces action and temporal coverage. We evaluate our framework on the MM-Fi dataset across three diverse experimental protocols. Source-code will be released upon acceptance. 

## 1 Introduction 

Three dimensional human pose estimation (3D HPE) is a central problem in computer vision, supporting applications in healthcare monitoring, rehabilitation, sports analysis, and human computer interaction. The majority of existing approaches operate on RGB video, benefiting from large scale image datasets and mature two stage pipelines that detect 2D keypoints before lifting them into 3D space [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9], with more recent methods incorporating temporal convolutions, transformers, kinematic priors, and diffusion based refinement [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. However, RGB model deployment remains limited in unconstrained settings such as hospitals and assisted living environments due to performance degradation under occlusions, poor illumination, viewpoint variations and privacy-concerns. [2, 16]. Because no single modality is reliable across all conditions, multimodal systems combining RGB, LiDAR, and millimeter-wave radar have emerged to leverage their complementary strengths [17, 18, 19]. However, effectively inte-grating these heterogeneousness modalities remains challenging. It requires resolving the differing feature distributions and noise characteristics of RGB, LiDAR, and radar into a common semantic space, while retaining the complementary, modality-specific evidence each sensor provides [20, 21, 17, 19]. A second, underexplored, concern in this setting is privacy. Human pose data captured through RGB, LiDAR, or radar can be linked back to individual subjects, yet the computer vision community has repeatedly been shown to overlook or mischaracterize this risk [22, 23, 24]. Differential privacy (DP) provides a principled and mathematically grounded framework [25] but applying it to visual prediction tasks via DP stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD) incurs substantial utility loss [26, 27, 28, 29]. Moreover, standard record-level DP protects individual samples but not the repeated participation of a subject across many records. This is especially problematic for LiDAR and radar-based human motion, whose four-dimensional spatio-temporal sequences are strongly correlated across frames from the same subject, so record-level guarantees can substantially understate an individual’s true privacy risk [30, 31]. Despite growing interest in both multimodal pose estimation and privacy preserving learning, the two problems have largely been studied in isolation. Audits of what a trained multimodal pose model reveals about its subjects remain arXiv:2610.02943v1 [cs.CV] 2 Oct 2026 Running Title for Header scarce, with prior privacy risk analysis in this space typically framed as membership or attribute inference under record level assumptions [32, 28]. This leaves open the question of how to design a multimodal pose estimation framework whose privacy is assessed and enforced at the level of the subject rather than the observation, and whether the resulting protection can be quantified beyond aggregate membership accuracy. In this work, we present a unified framework that couples kinematics induced multimodal fusion with subject level privacy auditing and private training for 3D-HPE. Our contributions are threefold: i) First, we introduce a kinematics-induced fusion module featuring a dual-track design with adaptive gating that aligns modality-specific joint represen-tations from RGB, LiDAR, and radar, and injects skeletal structure through fixed anatomical connectivity combined with learned joint dependencies. It adaptively aggregates the resulting evidence into a shared representation that outperforms each individual modality and achieves state-of-the-art performance. ii) Second, we formulate a black box subject level membership inference attack tailored to the regression nature of HPE and complement it with an empirical pointwise maximal leakage analysis incorporating Dirichlet smoothing [33], which characterizes how individual attack outcomes shift an adversary’s belief about subject membership, offering a finer grained view of leakage than threshold based attack metrics alone. iii) Third, we instantiate differential privacy at the subject rather than the sample level, coupled with private quantile-based adaptive clipping , following the general user level formulation of [31] and the correlated sampling perspective of [30]. Furthermore, we introduce Action Temporal Stratification , a population weighted within subject sampling strategy that preserves an unbiased subject gradient while enforcing coverage across actions and time. 

## 2 Related Work 

2.1 3D HPE using multimodal sensors 

RGB based 3D HPE has become a central research direction in computer vision, largely driven by the availability of large scale image and video datasets and well established 2D pose detectors. Early approaches typically adopted a two-stage pipeline: first detecting 2D keypoints, then lifting these 2D poses into 3D space[3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9], with recent work using temporal convolutions, transformers, kinematic priors, and diffusion models to model spatial-temporal pose structure [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. Despite strong benchmark performance, RGB-based methods remain sensitive to occlusion, illumination, resolution, viewpoint changes, and scene clutter because they do not directly observe 3D geometry. LiDAR and mmWave Radar have been explored as complementary sensing modalities for 3D HPE. LiDAR provides explicit 3D geometry and illumination invariance, enabling long-range markerless motion capture and robust 3D HPE from point clouds [34, 35, 36, 37]. However, LiDAR point clouds of humans are often sparse, incomplete, and sensitive to distance, self-occlusion, and sensor placement. mmWave Radar offers additional advantages under poor lighting and partial occlusion, and has been used for skeletal pose estimation, mesh recovery, diffusion-based pose estimation, and multi-view sensing [38, 39, 40, 41, 42]. Yet Radar point clouds remain extremely sparse, noisy, and anatomically coarse compared with RGB and LiDAR observations. Multimodal 3D HPE aims to combine these complementary cues for improved robustness and accuracy. Prior work has explored RGB-LiDAR pose estimation and multimodal human sensing datasets such as mRI and MM-Fi, which provide synchronized sensor streams for evaluating individual modalities and fusion strategies [17, 18, 19]. However, most multimodal systems require multiple sensors during inference, increasing hardware cost, calibration complexity, synchronization overhead, and deployment constraints. This motivates teacher-student settings in which a multimodal teacher learns from complementary sensors during training, while a single-modality student performs efficient inference using only one available sensor. 

2.2 Privacy-preservation in computer vision 

Growing evidence indicates that privacy preservation within computer vision community has frequently been ignored or misunderstood [24, 23]. The field has largely relied on fragmented, improvised anonymization techniques lacking formal guarantees. Rigorous alternatives such as differential privacy (DP) offer mathematical bounds, yet applying standard DP stochastic gradient descent to visual prediction tasks incurs a substantial utility loss [26]. Vision models suffer due to severe long-tail class imbalance that amplifies noise on minority classes, ie. rare poses, high dimensionality that reduces the signal-to-noise ratio of the gradient vectors [29, 27], and continuous pixel values that create non-smooth loss landscapes violating Lipschitz continuity [29]. Consequently, DP-SGD performance relies critically on loss smoothness, dimensionality, and clipping calibration [43, 28]. Furthermore, it has been increasingly recognized that standard record level DP is insufficient when training data contains strong temporal correlations or multi sample contributions from individual subjects [30, 31, 44]. ## 3 Methodology 

3.1 Overall Architecture 

The proposed architecture employs a heterogeneous modality specific encoders followed by a kinematics induced multimodal fusion module for 3D HPE. We utilize heterogeneous backbones to extract modality-specific joint rep-resentations: a DSTFormer-based encoder for RGB derived 2D keypoint sequences, and PointTransformerV2-based encoders for LiDAR and Radar point clouds. The modality specific representations are subsequently integrated through our kinematics induced fusion module for multimodal 3D HPE. This module uniquely maintains a modality specific joint representations together with shared fusion tokens, refining modality specific features through skeletal priors while simultaneously updating the shared representation through adaptive gating. This hierarchical design allows modality contributions to vary across anatomical joints and input samples through joint wise adaptive fusion weights. To preserve articulated structure, learnable joint queries are processed by a transformer-based keypoint head to produce one token for each of the J = 17 body joints [45]. The multimodal model predicts the final 3D pose from these joint tokens using a regression head. The overall architecture is given in Figure 1.

3.3 Action Temporal Stratified Subject-Level Differential Privacy 

Multi-modal human pose dataset contain many observations contributed by the same subject, including temporally overlapping windows within individual actions. A record level DP mechanism that treats each temporal window as an independent privacy unit protects the inclusion or removal of one window, but does not directly protect the inclusion or removal of a subject’s complete contribution. Towards this, we instantiate subject level differential privacy where we treat each subject’s entire records as the privacy unit following the general user wise formulation of [31] and prior user level private training with multiple samples per user [30]. For our setting, all the multi-modal pose windows belonging to one subject constitute a single privacy unit.

We evaluate membership evidence using the same subject-level units from private training. While membership inference measures whether an adversary can distinguish training from non training subjects, pointwise maximal leakage (PML) characterizes how individual score outcomes shift membership beliefs.

Bs = 1{s ∈ S tr }. (22) Membership only denotes inclusion in the training dataset. The attacker has prediction only access to the fixed final model fθT and Q query windows from the target subject, together with the 3D pose annotations. The adversary does not have access to model parameters, gradients, intermediate features, or metadata that would reveal any membership. This is a ground truth aware black-box threat model. ## 5 Conclusion 

In this work we present a framework for multimodal 3D human pose estimation that combines kinematics-induced fusion with subject-level DP training, and empirical membership inference. Overall, our fusion model improves the pose accuracy over individual modalities. The proposed Action Temporal Stratification constructs population-weighted subject gradients, while membership inference and PML provide complementary evidence of model privacy. The results show utility degradation under subject-level DP, particularly at stricter privacy budgets. Our evaluation is limited to the predefined MM-Fi split of subjects with eight non-member subjects. Future work should investigate more effective private adaptation techniques and evaluate on broader subject cohorts.

## 6 Appendix 

6.1 Experimental Setup Dataset and Implementation: We utilize MM-Fi [19], a comprehensive multimodal dataset designed for non-intrusive 4D human sensing. The dataset comprises over 320K synchronized frames across five modalities: RGB, depth, LiDAR, mmWave Radar, and WiFi CSI over 40 subjects. It encompasses 27 action categories, consisting of 14 daily activities and 13 rehabilitation activities. We specifically use LiDAR, Radar and RGB modalities for our experiments. MM-Fi is particularly challenging for Radar human pose estimation due to its sparse Radar point clouds containing fewer than 100 points per frame compared to other benchmarks. To test the robustness of our approach, we adopt the dataset’s three standard splits: random split, cross-subject split, and cross-environment split across all three protocols. We implement all our models on two NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs. We use PointTransformerV2 [53] as backbone for LiDAR and Radar point clouds while DSTFormer [13] serves as backbone for RGB based model. Additionally, in the RGB setting we follow the two stage pipeline of generating 3D pose from 2D estimates. MM-Fi dataset consists of 2D keypoints in prior extracted from HRNet-48 backbone and we leverage this. We use Adam optimizer for our experiments with a batch size of 32. The total epochs are set to 50. The learning rate is set to 1e-3 which is then multiplied by 0.1 after 40 epochs. We report Mean Per Joint Position Error (MPJPE) and Procrustes-aligned MPJPE (PA-MPJPE), following the standard 3D HPE evaluation protocols. Private models are evaluated only on MM-Fi protocol 3 under cross subject split with 32 training subjects treated as members and 8 held out subjects treated as non members.
