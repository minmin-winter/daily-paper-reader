## ABSTRACT 

A multimodal large language model that correctly reports a spatial fact does not necessarily use that fact in subsequent reasoning. To study this distinction, we in-troduce S PACE CONFLICT , a benchmark of 23,196 inputs for the construction and use of spatial state. Under a unified Supported/Contradictory/Unknown judgment interface, it covers local fact binding (L1), relational composition (L2), cross-observation consistency (L3), and state judgment under transformation (L4). Pos-ing a direct-state query, a full-transformation query, and an explicit-initial-state query on the same world reveals an availability–utilization gap: models recover the initial state from visual evidence yet fail when that state must drive a transfor-mation. For Qwen3.5-9B, 50 of 100 sequences with a correctly recovered initial state fail the full transformation, and supplying the state explicitly repairs all 50; the gap narrows with scale but does not close. We therefore propose Operational State Supervision (OSS), which supervises task-relevant spatial states and their transformation trajectories and aligns shared facts across contexts. OSS improves paired accuracy on matched judgments most on L3 and L4, the levels that depend on organizing and using state. Evaluating multimodal spatial reasoning thus re-quires asking not only whether a model can see and state a spatial fact, but whether that fact becomes a usable state in subsequent computation. Code and benchmark artifacts are available at https://github.com/changcv2021/Seein g-Saying-but-Not-Using-From-Reportable-Spatial-Facts -to-Usable-States-in-Multimodal-Models .

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have made rapid progress in visual understanding and spatial reasoning. However, spatial intelligence requires more than recognizing objects or reporting pairwise spatial relations. An agent operating in a changing environment must recover spatial facts from visual evidence, organize observations into a coherent world state, and continue to compute over that state as actions, time, or hypothetical interventions alter the scene. This reveals a distinction that can be obscured by end-to-end accuracy: a model may correctly report a spatial fact when asked directly, yet fail when that same fact must serve as the starting point for subsequent computation. Existing benchmarks have broadened both the observation settings and the task complexity of spatial evaluation: VSI-Bench Yang et al. (2025) tests memory and reasoning over layouts from continuous video; Spatial457 Wang et al. (2025) grades tasks from object recognition to 3D/6D relations; SPAR-Bench Zhang et al. (2026) spans perception to higher-order reasoning in single- and multi-view settings; and Hypo3D Mao et al. (2025) asks for spatial states after a described scene change. All of them, however, score the final spatial judgment. An end-to-end score collapses fact recovery and answer selection into a single prediction, so an error does not reveal whether the required state was never recovered or was recovered but not used. We introduce SpaceConflict, a multimodal benchmark designed around controlled conflicts among visual evidence, spatial claims, and changing world states. Rather than treating spatial reasoning

Together, these levels trace a progression from spatial facts to an operational world state. The explicit state trajectory in L4 allows us to query, within the same world, the initial state, the complete transformation, and the same transformation when the initial state is explicitly provided, thereby separating whether a fact can be recovered from whether it can be used. To our knowl-edge, SpaceConflict is the first multimodal benchmark to combine controlled spatial conflicts with sequential state transformations while explicitly distinguishing fact reportability from downstream state usability. Using this interface, we identify a pronounced availability–utilization gap. Qwen3.5-9B correctly recovers the initial state under an independent query in 100 of 160 sequences, yet fails the corresponding full transformation in 50 of those cases. Explicitly supplying the correct initial state recovers all 50 failures. The gap becomes smaller as model size increases but does not disap-pear. Further controlled experiments narrow the explanation for this phenomenon. When the same transformations are provided symbolically, models solve nearly all cases, indicating that the gap therefore cannot be attributed to an inability to execute the transformation itself. Rewording the query repairs one class of state-selection errors in the symbolic setting, whereas the visual-task gap appears under fixed wording and is repaired by supplying the state rather than rewriting the query. In non-count relation tasks, placing the same state value in the context as an irrelevant register does not reproduce the benefit of supplying it as the explicit initial state. Directly injecting candidate state information into the tested local activation sites likewise fails to reproduce this benefit. Together, these results indicate that the central issue is not only whether spatial information is present, but whether it is integrated into subsequent reasoning in the appropriate computational role. Motivated by this diagnosis, we introduce Operational State Supervision (OSS). Rather than supervising only the final answer, OSS directly supervises task-relevant spatial states and their transformation trajec-tories, while aligning spatial facts shared across different reasoning contexts. Its gains concentrate on L3 and L4, where state organization and use are most central, and it substantially improves paired judgment accuracy (PairAcc). SpaceConflict therefore exposes a spatial reasoning problem that is different from simply “knowing the answer”: a model may be able to observe and report a spatial fact without being able to use that fact reliably in subsequent computation. Our contributions are threefold: 1. We introduce SpaceConflict, a four-level benchmark that traces spatial reasoning from reportable facts to operational states. 2. We identify a behavioral availability– utilization gap: spatial facts that models can report correctly often fail to serve reliably as the starting state for subsequent computation. 3. We propose Operational State Supervision (OSS) , which di-rectly supervises task-relevant states and their transformation trajectories to strengthen the transition from reportable spatial facts to operational states. 

## 2 THE SPACE CONFLICT BENCHMARK 

SpaceConflict evaluates whether multimodal models can distinguish spatial claims that agree with an observed world from closely matched claims that contradict it. Its central design principle is to vary the spatial semantics of a claim while holding the visual evidence and task context fixed. As shown in Figure 1, S PACE CONFLICT organizes four structural reasoning requirements with progressively increasing dependence on state information under a unified claim-judgment interface, and further asks: can a directly recoverable spatial fact reliably support a subsequent state transformation? 2.1 EVIDENCE -C ONDITIONED SPATIAL JUDGMENTS 

We define a world w as a set of source-grounded observations that share the same underlying scene identity. Depending on the organization of the source dataset, a world may correspond to a single-image scene, a capture, a parent video, or the base scene of a state transformation. The same world may produce multiple viewpoints, temporal segments, task contexts, and derived claims. Thus, one world may give rise to multiple evidence packages E, while each individual model request observes only one of them. For a specific model request, we select the evidence visible for the current task from world w and define the evidence package as E = ( M, K ), where M denotes the model-visible visual evidence, including a single image, multiple views, or temporally indexed media, and K

denotes the task context relevant to the current judgment, such as a specified viewpoint, temporal scope, reference frame, or model-visible state transformation. A model input consists of an evidence package E and a spatial claim c: (E, c ). The model classifies the claim as Supported, Contradictory, Unknown These labels indicate, respectively, that the claim is supported by the available evidence, explicitly conflicts with the evidence, or cannot be determined from the available evidence. 2.3 SOURCE -G ROUNDED CONSTRUCTION 

SPACE CONFLICT extracts verifiable spatial facts from existing spatial data sources through deter-ministic adapters, including structured annotations, metadata, and question–answer records; each adapter normalizes only the information explicitly supported by its source. The specific role and contract of each source are described in Appendix A. Based on these structured facts, we first iden-tify a claim c+ supported by the current evidence and then apply a predefined semantic edit to obtain 

c−. Each edit modifies only a single field or a fixed set of fields, such as swapping the subject and object, while leaving all remaining fields unchanged. A claim pair is realized only when the source facts and permitted inference rules explicitly support c+ and refute c−. The two claims are then generated under a shared language-realization plan, ensuring that their contrast arises from a change in spatial meaning rather than from differences in evidence conditions or surface form. The overall pipeline can therefore be summarized as source evidence → structured facts → controlled semantic edit → matched claims. Different levels impose different dependencies on these structured facts. For example, L1 can construct local conflicts through entity rebinding or a subject–object swap; L2 currently relies primarily on source-authorized transitivity to construct two-premise relation chains that cannot be resolved from either premise alone; and L3 uses multiview, appearance-order, or tem-poral interval information to construct cross-observation conflicts. The complete operator set and level coverage are provided in Appendix B. L4 further distinguishes the provenance of the post-state. Among the current 1,136 Supported/Contradictory pairs, 416 are Native: their post-state facts are derived from source-provided context changes and the corresponding post-state question–answer records. The remaining 720 are Controlled: the benchmark applies model-visible discrete transfor-mations to source-grounded pre-states and derives local post-states through deterministic transition procedures. Pre-state grounding for the Controlled branches combines the available source annota-tions for objects, counts, and spatial relations. The specific sources, geometric rules, and reference-frame conventions are described in Appendix B.5. For Controlled branches, two independently implemented transition procedures compute the local post-state and are required to agree. This dual implementation check validates the deterministic state transition itself. Both branches use the same L4 model interface described in Section 2.2; only the provenance of the post-state differs. Complete L4 construction, provenance, and validation protocols are provided in Appendix B.5. 

Provenance and validation. Validation applies checks appropriate to the corresponding source, level, and label type, including local refutation, compositional premise verification, cross-observation dependency checks, and post-state verification. The same structured records also pro-vide the task-relevant state targets used by the subsequent diagnostics and OSS training. Complete record formats, validation scope, and measured coverage are described in Appendix C. Evaluation and Data Protocols. The primary task is three-way spatial-claim classification. We report two metrics: ClaimAcc , the accuracy over all claims including Unknown, and PairAcc , the fraction of matched Supported/Contradictory pairs in which both claims are judged correctly; PairAcc is computed only over paired claims and is the stricter measure. Model-generated explanations are evaluated only as auxiliary outputs; scoring details are given in Appendix E.1. The benchmark contains 4,284 worlds, with per-level pair and Unknown counts in Table 1 and data splits in Ap-pendix D.3. The diagnostics use matched state queries constructed separately from the benchmark claims (Appendix E.2).

## 4 OPERATIONAL STATE SUPERVISION 

The diagnosis suggests two supervision targets that answer-only training does not provide. First, the same spatial fact appears in different roles—as an atomic relation in L1, as part of a world state in L3, as the initial state of a transformation in L4—and correct reporting in one role does not transfer to another. We therefore assign a fact the same canonical target wherever it appears (cross-context state alignment). Second, the effect of an explicit initial state indicates that the weak link is the connection between the grounded state and the computation that consumes it. The deterministic L4 trajectory lets us supervise the state at each step (state-trajectory supervision). OSS implements both as auxiliary examples added to fine-tuning, leaving the inference interface unchanged. 4.1 CANONICAL STATE REPRESENTATION 

We represent each annotated spatial fact as a canonical tuple: τ = ( e, a, v ), where e denotes an entity or entity pair, a denotes the relevant state variable or spatial relation, and v denotes its value. When required by the task, τ is augmented with additional fields such as the reference frame. A task-relevant state may contain one or more such facts: Φ( S) = {τj }m(S)

Table 3 summarizes the complete results of all evaluated models on S PACE CONFLICT . We make three main observations. First, different models exhibit clearly different capability pro-files across the four structural reasoning requirements. Qwen3.6-27B achieves the highest Overall ClaimAcc/PairAcc ( 65 .16 /52 .25% ) and the strongest claim-level performance on L1–L3. How-ever, Qwen3.5-27B obtains higher PairAcc on some levels, while GPT-5.4 performs best on L4. Thus, stronger aggregate performance does not imply consistent superiority across different spa-tial reasoning requirements. Second, L1–L4 do not form a monotonically increasing empirical difficulty ladder. For example, Qwen3.5-9B improves from 55 .45 /39 .08% ClaimAcc/PairAcc on L1 to 89 .81 /84 .57% on L2, but drops to 53 .65 /39 .58% on L3 and 60 .73 /30 .81% on L4. Simi-lar non-monotonic profiles are observed across other models, supporting our interpretation of the four levels as distinct structural requirements: local fact binding, relational composition, cross-observation consistency, and transformation-conditioned state judgment. Third, PairAcc reveals consistency failures that are not apparent from ClaimAcc alone. Qwen3.5-9B and Qwen3.6-27B achieve L4 ClaimAcc scores of 60 .73% and 65 .05% , respectively, while their matched-pair PairAcc scores are only 30 .81% and 39 .79% . Because PairAcc requires both members of the same Sup-ported/Contradictory pair to be judged correctly, it provides a stricter measure of consistency under controlled spatial conflicts. ClaimAcc additionally includes Unknown examples, whereas PairAcc is computed only over complete Supported/Contradictory pairs; the two metrics therefore provide complementary rather than directly comparable views of model behavior. These results motivate a more controlled analysis of L4. Aggregate L4 performance alone cannot distinguish two differ-ent sources of failure: whether the correct initial state is unavailable (availability), or whether a recoverable state is not reliably used in the subsequent transformation (utilization). 5.3 RESULTS OF THE L4 D IAGNOSIS

## 7 CONCLUSION 

We presented S PACE CONFLICT , a benchmark that tests, under one claim-judgment interface, whether multimodal models can carry spatial facts from local binding through cross-observation consistency to judgments about transformed states. Controlled diagnostics reveal an availability– utilization gap: facts a model reports correctly often do not enter its subsequent state computation, and supplying the initial state repairs most such failures. OSS extends supervision from final an-swers to task-relevant states and their transformation trajectories, and its gains concentrate in L3 and L4. A spatial fact can thus be seen and said, yet still fail to become a state the model can use.

B.2 L1: A TOMIC FACT JUDGMENTS 

Atomicity is defined at the claim level. A supported or contradictory L1 claim is decided using one independent source-grounded fact and locally authorized rules, without conjunction of independent factual premises. Local rule application can include both inversion and exclusivity; it is not restricted to exactly one rule. The positive and negative claims may use different deciding facts. For example, the original relation of one chair can support c+, while the relation of a second chair refutes an entity-rebound c−. E.1 CLAIM -L EVEL AND PAIR -L EVEL SCORING

20 Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 Here the pipe-separated label string describes the allowed alternatives; an actual response contains exactly one label. ClaimAcc and PairAcc. Complete pairs alone enter PairAcc, and a pair is correct only when both separately generated labels match their respective gold labels. The original E0 protocol retains at most 512 generated tokens. Its label scorer uses the retained output: a recoverable valid label is not discarded solely because a trailing explanation or confidence field is incomplete. The benchmark label parser is distinct from the typed value parser used in state diagnostics. Explanation scores are auxiliary automatic assessments and do not enter ClaimAcc or PairAcc. 

Additional metrics. BinaryClaimAcc restricts the individual-claim denominator to the Sup-ported/Contradictory subset. Contradictory and Unknown recall condition on the corresponding gold labels. World-macro accuracy averages per-world accuracies, rather than counting entirely correct worlds. When confidence intervals accompany the original E0 results, they use the stored source-stratified, world-cluster bootstrap with 5,000 resamples. Training-run comparisons retain their declared protocol and grouping. ClaimAcc and PairAcc measure different events and can use different example populations because ClaimAcc includes Unknown. Their numerical difference is not by itself an estimate of within-pair error dependence. In particular, ClaimAcc squared is not the relevant independence reference when the Supported and Contradictory marginal accuracies differ or Unknown examples enter ClaimAcc. Such dependence analyses require the two marginals computed on the same complete-pair popula-tion. E.2 BENCHMARK CLAIMS VERSUS DERIVED STATE DIAGNOSTICS 

The release evaluates supported, contradictory, and underdetermined claims. The behavioral analy-sis additionally constructs matched state queries from selected source worlds. Direct-state queries ask for a task-relevant initial value; full-transition queries provide the visual evidence and transfor-mation descriptions and ask for the final value; explicit-state queries additionally supply the correct initial value. These queries are separate model requests and use the diagnostic value-scoring inter-face, not the E0 claim classifier. The primary count panel contains 160 sequences from 80 source worlds. Each world has an inverse sequence and a two-step sequence; the inverse returns to its initial count, so the supplied initial value also equals the final value on that trajectory. The counting scope remains the reference image, rather than the union of support views. A separate 47-world relation panel covers 34 horizontal, 6 vertical, and 7 depth cases using an axis reflection followed by identity. These diagnostic panels are not additional inputs in the 24,396-claim release and do not redefine the original level membership of their source worlds. The distinction matters for the progression from benchmark to diagnosis and training. The bench-mark provides scoped, source-linked judgments; the controlled panels expose initial- and resulting-state queries; and auxiliary supervision is constructed only where the relevant state values and up-dates are supported by the corresponding annotations. A shared schema does not imply that every world supplies every level, a complete scene graph, or a fully annotated multistep trajectory.

## P INTERPRETATION BOUNDARIES AND UNTESTED ALTERNATIVES 

The experimental findings support a distinction between independently reportable facts and success on matched transformation queries. They do not show that the correct initial state was present in the failed run’s internal computation, nor do they locate a unique perception, binding, initialization, transition, or readout failure. Oracle input changes information availability and linguistic task orga-nization at the same time. W1 demonstrates this sensitivity for one symbolic query anomaly; it does not eliminate every prompt-related explanation for B1 or the non-count tasks. 43 Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 The explicit-state contrast is not uniform across models and relations. 4B includes harmed count successes and lacks the overall positive non-count explicit-minus-sham result; 9B’s depth subset is a counterexample to its aggregate non-count improvement. The non-count program contains only one nontrivial reflection. Consequently, neither the test scale nor the state operations support a claim about arbitrary three-dimensional dynamics or embodied planning. Representation readability and causal efficacy are different measurements. The prespecified nega-tive probe comparison coexists with more readable variables and oracle contexts in the full ledger. I2 does not validate the specified intermediate-state interchange, and I1 does not pass its frozen val-idation gate. Non-significant intervals are not equivalence tests proving zero effect. Limited donor coverage, engine-equivalence exclusions, small cluster counts, and the chosen single-token interven-tion family remain material limits. Conversely, no damage without successful rescue is not positive evidence for selective repair. The evidence report distinguishes automatic provisional review from human verification, and no new human verification is claimed for these diagnostics. All-split coverage, existing test-only scores, diagnostic discovery worlds, and window-validation partitions have different roles. Post-hoc de-scriptive family and case summaries remain labeled as such. The results below retain negative, zero, and unrun outcomes; they do not infer a compact state circuit or a distributed replacement merely to supply a unified mechanism story.
