## Abstract 

Multi-modal large language models (MLLMs) achieve strong modality understand-ing by pairing a large language model (LLM) with an encoder for a target modality such as vision, video, or audio. However, improving an MLLM’s capability for a given modality typically requires additional training on large modality-specific datasets, incurring substantial data collection and compute costs. Model merging offers an alternative, but it is often infeasible for data-scarce, large per-sample size, or domain-specific modalities ( e.g. , audio and video), where same-modality model variants are rarely available. In this work, we characterize an intriguing asymmetric phenomenon: merging a well-aligned, data-rich source-modality MLLM into a data-scarce target-modality MLLM substantially improves the target on its own benchmarks. Our theoretical and empirical analyses show that this gain stems from enhanced alignment between modality-specific and textual tokens, induced by the stronger donor modality. Specifically, we derive a mutual-information lower bound that is monotonic in alignment-related quantities and strongly correlated with downstream MLLM performance. Building on this principle, we propose Directional Cross-modal Alignment Transfer (DCAT), a novel framework that transfers textual alignment from a strong, well-aligned source (donor) modality to a weak target (recipient) modality, boosting target-modality performance without further fine-tuning. We further show that the alignment-enhancing objective ad-mits a closed-form weight-space solution computed from only a small calibration set. DCAT outperforms existing model-merging methods, offering an efficient path toward cross-modal alignment transfer. Project page with code is available at

## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) [3, 41, 54, 67, 70] achieve strong performance across textual tasks through scaling in data and model size. Most existing multi-modal LLMs (MLLMs) [12, 42, 81] have advanced visual reasoning, video understanding, or audio recognition by pairing a strong LLM backbone with a single modality encoder such as vision [52, 68, 88], video [35, 39, 46] or audio [53, 59, 62] encoders, and a projector [36, 42] that aligns the modality with the textual space. However, enhancing the target-modality capability of an MLLM typically requires additional training on large modality-specific data, incurring substantial data collection and compute costs. Model merging [9, 17, 28, 48, 75, 82] offers a promising alternative for improving MLLMs by com-bining same-architecture models with little data. For vision MLLMs, multiple same-backbone variants and abundant modality data are often available and can be merged to enhance visual capabilities.

However, such data abundance and same-modality variants are much scarcer for other domain-specific modalities, where training multiple variants is costly or infeasible. While existing studies have mostly focused on merging within a single modality, merging different-modality MLLMs remains largely unexplored. We argue that MLLMs trained for different modalities may still share the same base LLM, making cross-modality weight merging possible. Thus, we ask a more targeted question: can a source-modality MLLM (donor) transfer useful alignment to a target-modality MLLM (recipient), thereby improving the recipient’s target-modality performance without additional training? In this paper, we identify and study an intriguing and re-curring phenomenon: merging a donor and a recipient via existing merging methods can yield substantial perfor-mance gains over the original recipient on its associated benchmark. We investigate this phenomenon along two complementary axes. On the theoretical side, we analyze it through the lens of mutual information (MI) [31, 61]. Under a mild assumption, we prove that a lower bound on the MI between text and the other modality is mono-tonic in the product of two quantities: the Subspace Energy Overlap (SEO), which measures the extent to which each modality’s token energy lies within the text-aligned sub-space, and the Spectral Diversity (SD) [56], which mea-sures how uniformly that energy is distributed within this subspace. We refer to this product as the Alignment score. On the empirical side, across hundreds of model check-points spanning multiple architectures, we conduct a series of statistically tested analyses and find that the Alignment score is strongly and significantly correlated with MLLM performance, and that merged models exhibiting perfor-mance gains also show a statistically significant increase in the Alignment. Taken together, these theoretical and empir-ical results support our MI-lower-bound-based Alignment score, as a plausible explanatory mechanism for the observed benefits of model merging. Building on these analyses, we propose Directional Cross-modal Alignment Transfer (DCAT), a novel framework that transfers alignment from a high-alignment donor MLLM to a recipient MLLM using only a few hundred calibration samples, thereby improving the recipient’s target-modality performance. To our knowledge, we are the first to formulate and analyze this phenomenon; accordingly, the most natural baselines for DCAT are the existing model-merging methods through which the phenomenon is initially observed. As illustrated in Fig. 1, we instantiate this directional transfer by using vision MLLMs, which generally exhibit high alignment, as donors and audio or video MLLMs as recipients. Across a diverse suite of benchmarks, including MMAU [69], AIR-Bench [84], Video-MME [16], and MVBench [37], DCAT achieves 29.55% improvement for audio tasks and 24.92% for video tasks on average over the original recipient models. Our contributions can be summarized as follows: • We investigate an asymmetric phenomenon: model merging between MLLMs trained on different modalities improves target-modality performance. We introduce a theory-motivated alignment metric that explains this phenomenon, supported by strong empirical analysis. • Building on this analysis, we propose Directional Cross-modal Alignment Transfer (DCAT), a method that leverages a small amount of data to perform a closed-form weight update, thereby transferring alignment from a well-aligned modality to a relatively weaker one. • We conduct extensive experiments across multiple MLLMs on various benchmarks. DCAT consistently outperforms existing model merging methods and achieves average performance gains of 29.55% over the base model on audio tasks and 24.92% on video tasks.

Model merging. As neural networks scale, deploying separate task specialists becomes prohibitive, motivating model merging as a training-free way to consolidate independently fine-tuned models. TA [28] enables model editing via task vectors, defined as weight differences between fine-tuned and pretrained models. ## 3 Analysis 

3.1 Model merging increases modality performance 

While task-wise model merging, which combines LLMs fine-tuned on different downstream tasks into one model, has been extensively studied, comparatively little attention has been paid to merging MLLMs trained for different input modalities. Tab. 1 reports MMAU [69] scores for vision–audio MLLM merges and Video-MME [16] scores for vision–video MLLM merges. All checkpoints are from OptMerge [75] which are Vicuna [10]-based. Despite modality heterogeneity, merging with a vision MLLM improves audio and video benchmark performance by up to 18.45% over the original modality-specific MLLM. This raises a natural question: what drives this striking performance gain?

## 5 Experiment 

5.1 Experimental settings Baselines. Although first observed in model merging, DCAT has a distinct goal: transferring stronger donor alignment to a recipient rather than equipping one MLLM with multiple tasks or modalities. Because both settings combine models at the weight level, we compare DCAT with repre-sentative merging baselines: Task Arithmetic [28] (TA), TIES-merging [82] (TIES), ISO-C [48] (ISO), Task Singular Vector [17] (TSV), WUDI [9], and OptMerge [75]. Experiments cover Audio+Vision and Video+Vision settings on two widely used backbones, Vicuna [10] and LLaMA 3.1 [19]. For Vicuna, we use the weights released by OptMerge. For LLaMA 3.1, we use in-the-wild models that share the same architecture but are independently trained with different recipes, demonstrating DCAT’s robustness and practical utility. Full baseline details are in the Appendix Sec. D.2. 

Benchmarks. We extensively compare DCAT’s alignment transfer performance across audio and video benchmarks. For audio, we evaluate on MMAU [69] (1K samples), AIR-Bench Foun-dation [84] (25K samples), MuCho-music [74] (1.2K samples), Clotho-AQA [40] (1.4K samples), and VocalSound [18] (3.6K samples). For video, we evaluate on Video-MME [16] (2.7K samples), TempCompass [44] (5.5K samples), MVBench [37] (4K samples), and MLVU [91] (2.2K samples). Subcategory-level results are provided in the Appendix Sec. F.2. 

Implementation details. DCAT progressively merges models layer by layer from the first to the last layer. At each layer, our closed-form update is applied to all linear projections, aligning internal representations across computational modules. For calibration, we use AVQA [83], which supports both audio and video: audio tracks calibrate audio MLLMs, and video tracks calibrate video MLLMs, using 256 samples each. Remaining hyperparameters, including the text subspace dimension k,cross-modal alignment weight η, and anchor parameter β, are detailed in Appendix C. 

5.2 Quantitative comparison 

To evaluate how effectively DCAT boosts modality performance, we compare it against various model merging methods. ## 6 Discussion 

As shown in Fig. 2, we consistently observe that vision models suffer a performance drop when merging is applied, and this trend holds across multiple models and architectures. Accordingly, most of our experiments employ a vision model as the donor and MLLMs of other modalities as recipients for alignment transfer. We offer the following conjecture as to why the donor has consistently been a vision model: because visual data is easier to obtain and far more abundant than audio or video data, existing vision MLLMs are likely to be more thoroughly aligned than their counterparts in other modalities. Under this hypothesis, DCAT is expected to leverage models trained on common modalities to enhance the performance of MLLMs built for relatively rare and data-scarce modalities. 

## 7 Conclusion 

In this paper, we report the phenomenon that inter-modality model merging can improve performance, and we interpret this effect from a Mutual Information (MI) perspective by deriving a lower bound on the MI and proposing a monotonic Alignment metric. An extensive empirical and statistical analysis demonstrates that this Alignment exhibits a strong correlation with downstream performance. Building on these theoretical and empirical findings, we propose Directional Cross-Modal Alignment Transfer (DCAT), which transfers alignment from a donor modality model to a recipient modality model in closed form. Across multiple audio and video MLLMs, we validate DCAT on a wide range of benchmarks, achieving average relative improvements of 29.55% on audio and 24.92% on video over the original models, thereby substantiating the soundness and the effectiveness of DCAT. 9Acknowledgment

B.5 Empirical validation of δ

The proof of Theorem 1 involves a spectral non-degeneracy factor that connects the proof-side spectral term SD det with the entropy-based effective-rank measure SD used in the main text. In particular, the relevant condition can be expressed as the existence of a constant δ > 0 such that 

SD det ≥ δ SD . (A67) Equivalently, the largest empirical value supported by a given spectrum is 

δeff := SD det 

SD . (A68) Importantly, δeff is a spectral conversion factor between two spectral-spread measures, not a per-eigenvalue probability lower bound of the form pj ≥ δ/k . Therefore, δeff is not constrained to be at most one, and values larger than one do not contradict the probability constraint P 

> j

pj = 1 .For empirical validation, we compute both SD and SD det using the features examined in our analysis, and report their ratio δeff in Table A1. As shown in the table, δeff remains close to one in both settings: 

0.9704 for Audio+Vision and 1.1046 for Video+Vision. These results indicate that the conversion from SD det to the entropy-based SD incurs only a mild constant-level relaxation in the MI lower bound. Thus, although the main text adopts the more interpretable entropy-based effective-rank measure SD , the empirical gap between SD and SD det is small in our analyzed model families. This supports the use of SD as a practical and meaningful spectral diversity metric for the proposed Alignment score. 

## C Implementation Details 

C.1 Models and computation 

For the Vicuna, the setting allows a controlled comparison because the three MLLMs are trained from the same base under the same recipe except for the modality in OptMerge [75]. To complement this controlled setting, we additionally evaluate DCAT on independently trained models that nevertheless share the LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct backbones for audio, video, and vision modalities. For the two backbones, the vision MLLM is used as the donor in every experiment, since vision MLLMs are the modality on which standard merging tends to leave performance unchanged or slightly degraded while consistently lifting the other modalities. All experiments run on a single NVIDIA L40S 48 GB GPU and the DCAT solver over all projections completes in about 25-30 minutes per setting. 

C.2 Calibration and alignment-target data 

For all four settings, calibration data is drawn from the publicly available AVQA dataset [83] since the evaluation benchmarks (MMAU, Video-MME, etc.) are entirely disjoint from AVQA so that calibration and evaluation never share data. AVQA provides paired audio and video streams aligned to the same set of question/answer prompts, which lets us instantiate DCAT in a uniform way across audio and video recipients without changing the underlying source of Xtext . For audio recipients, we feed the audio track in AVQA to the recipient MLLM as the modality input; for video recipients, we instead feed the video clip of the same sample with the same prompt used for the audio track. C.3 Progressive, dependency-aware merging 

Although the closed-form update in Eq. 10 could in principle be solved for all projections in parallel, we found it preferable to apply the update progressively along two axes—across layers and across projection groups within each layer. Concretely, we process layers sequentially, and within each layer we partition the seven projections into four dependency-aware groups that mirror the activation-flow structure of a transformer block: { query,key,value } and { out } projections in the attention layers, {up,gate } and { down } projections in the attention projections and the feed-forward networks. The four groups are merged in the listed order, and after every group merge we re-collect Xmod and 

Xtext at the relevant point so that the next group is solved against activations that already reflect the previous update. In this way, errors that would arise from treating projections as independent are absorbed iteratively, and the alignment accumulated across layers is corrected as it propagates. 

C.4 Hyperparameter values 

In DCAT we should control three hyperparameters: the text-subspace dimension k, the recipient/donor balance β in the cross-modal alignment term, and the cross-modal alignment weight η. For example, the text-subspace dimension k is shared within a backbone family ( k = 128 for Vicuna and k = 256 

for LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct), reflecting that hidden states of LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct are wider and require a larger effective rank to span the principal text directions. The complete configuration is reported in Table A2.

## D Experimental Details 

D.1 Analysis details Alignment-accuracy Correlation Analysis. To examine whether the proposed alignment metric is correlated with model performance, we conduct a correlation analysis between alignment and benchmark accuracy using both Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients. The analysis is performed on Audio+Vision and Video+Vision settings, under Vicuna [10] and LLaMA 3.1 [19] backbones. For model merging, we include Task Arithmetic [28] (TA), TIES-merging [82] (TIES), ISO-C [48] (ISO), Task Singular Vector [17] (TSV), WUDI [9], and OptMerge [75]. For each merging method, we first construct a fully merged model by merging the Vision model with either the Audio or Video model. Because correlation analysis requires multiple evaluation points rather than a single merged checkpoint, we further generate additional model variants by 23 applying SVD-based low-rank decomposition to the fully merged network parameters. Specifically, starting from each full-merge checkpoint, we decompose the merged weights and reconstruct a series of low-rank approximations with ranks 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, and 128. We then evaluate both the full-merge model and these SVD-derived variants, and compute Pearson and Spearman correlations between their Alignment scores and benchmark accuracies. This allows us to analyze how changes in the merged parameter space are reflected in both alignment and task performance. The evaluation is conducted separately on the benchmark corresponding to each constituent modality. For example, in the Audio+Vision setting, we measure performance on both the audio benchmark and the vision benchmark. We use TextVQA [63] for vision, MMAU [69] for audio, and Video-MME [16] for video. In addition, we examine a broader set of benchmarks, whose results are reported in Sec. E.1 For each benchmark, we evaluate 300 samples that are equally sampled across categories to ensure balanced coverage. For video evaluation, we use tokens from only the first, middle, and last frames of each sample for computational efficiency, instead of processing all frames. To provide a reference for the effect of merging itself, we additionally evaluate the original modality-specific models, i.e., the models trained on a single modality without merging, on the corresponding benchmarks. These reference results allow us to determine whether merging improves or degrades performance relative to the original specialized models. For experiments, the proposed alignment metric is computed between modality tokens and text tokens at each backbone layer with k = 32 and γ = 0 .8, and the alignment values are averaged over all samples in the benchmark dataset to obtain a layer-wise alignment profile. Since alignment varies substantially across layers, we analyze the results in a layer-wise manner, as shown in Fig. 3. Consistent with prior observations that cross-modal alignment tends to be strongest in middle layers, we select the 21st layer as a representative layer and report the linear regression plots and correlation values based on this layer in Fig. 2.

D.2.2 Base models Vicuna-based MLLMs. We use the Vicuna-based modality-specific MLLMs released by Opt-Merge [75]. All models share Vicuna-7B-v1.5 as the language backbone, while employing modality-specific encoders and connectors: the vision-language model uses CLIP-ViT-L-336px [52] with an 25 MLP projector, the audio-language model uses BEATs-Iter3+ [45] with a Q-Former [36] connec-tor, and the video-language model uses LanguageBind [92] with an MLP projector. These models are trained on bi-modal modality-text data for vision, audio, and video, respectively, following a two-stage procedure consisting of connector alignment and subsequent fine-tuning. Following the original modality-merging setup, we preserve each modality-specific encoder and connector, and apply merging only to the shared language-model parameters. This setup allows us to evaluate whether cross-modality merging can transfer alignment across different modality-specific MLLMs while keeping the underlying language backbone fixed. 

LLaMA 3.1-based MLLMs. We further evaluate DCAT on LLaMA 3.1-based MLLMs collected from publicly available in-the-wild checkpoints. Specifically, we use LLaVA-LLaMA3.1-8B 2 as the vision-language model, Ultravox-v0.4.1-LLaMA-3.1-8B 3 as the audio-language model, and LLaVA-Video-LLaMA-3.1-8B 4 as the video-language model. Unlike the Vicuna-based models, these checkpoints are independently developed and trained with different data mixtures, training recipes, and modality-specific components, while sharing the same LLaMA 3.1-8B language-model architecture. For each model, we preserve its original modality encoder and connector, and apply merging only to the language-model parameters that are architecturally compatible across models. This setting provides a more practical and less controlled testbed for evaluating whether DCAT can robustly transfer cross-modal alignment across heterogeneous real-world MLLMs. 

Considered, but not experimented models. Our merging setup assumes that the modality-specific MLLMs share the same language-model architecture and parameter shapes, since both DCAT and the baseline merging methods operate directly at the weight level. We also considered other in-the-wild MLLMs that appear to share the same backbone family, such as Qwen-Audio [12] and LLaVA-Next [35] with Qwen-1.5 [3] backbones. However, these models often differ in tokenizer size, input embedding size, and LM head size, which prevents direct weight-space merging across all compared methods without additional architectural modification or retraining. Similarly, several recent public MLLMs, including Idefics3-8B-Llama3 5 and Llama-3.1-Nemotron-Nano-VL-8B-V1 6,were considered but excluded because their backbone implementations or parameter shapes are not fully compatible with the other modality-specific models. We did not introduce additional training or ad-hoc resizing procedures to force compatibility, as this would confound the comparison among training-free merging methods. To the best of our knowledge, our experiments cover the publicly available in-the-wild MLLMs that satisfy the required architectural compatibility conditions; we provide a more detailed discussion of this limitation in Sec. E.3 Rationale of scheduling α

DCAT adjusts α based on the relative changes in SEO and SD, as described in Eq. 11. This design is motivated by the layer-wise structure of the text subspace. As shown in Fig. A7, the effective rank of text-token activations generally decreases in the middle layers, although the degree of this trend varies across architectures and modalities. When the text subspace is effectively low-rank, aggressively increasing SD may spread modality information over weak or noisy text-space directions, leading to noisy alignment rather than reliable modality-text alignment. To mitigate this risk, DCAT uses the relative behavior of SEO and SD to adaptively schedule α, making the target more conservative when the reliable text subspace is limited. Empirically, this schedule induces an inverse relationship between the text effective rank and α. In layers where the text effective rank is small, α becomes larger, encouraging DCAT to emphasize SEO and preserve the recipient’s projected spectrum rather than over-promoting SD. Conversely, when the text effective rank is larger, α decreases and allows stronger encouragement of SD. Although the LLaMA 3.1-based audio model shows a relatively different effective-rank pattern compared with the other settings, the scheduled α still follows the intended inverse trend with respect to the text effective rank.

F.3 Results on vision benchmarks 

We use vision MLLMs as donor models to improve the performance of audio and video MLLMs. A natural question is how the vision performance of the resulting transferred models changes after DCAT. We investigate this question using Vicuna-based models, and report the results in Tab.A7. We evaluate on a diverse set of vision benchmarks, including GQA[25], MMBench [43], MMStar [8], POPE [38], and Text-VQA [63]. When the audio MLLM is used as the recipient, the vision performance is largely preserved or slightly improved, indicating only minor perturbations. In contrast, when the video MLLM is used as the recipient, we observe some degree of degradation in vision performance. This behavior is expected because DCAT transfers alignment rather than modality-specific capabilities. We emphasize that our goal is not to obtain a single model that performs well on both modalities, but rather to transfer the capability of a strongly aligned MLLM from one modality to another in order to improve performance on the target modality.

F.8 Additional ablation study 

In this section, in addition to the component-level ablation studies conducted in Tab. 3, we present ablation studies on key hyperparameters. Specifically, we study the balancing coefficient β in

For each hyperparameter, we select the sweep range of the parameters to the values where marked performance degradation is observed on target-modality benchmarks such as MMAU and Video-MME. ## G Limitation 

First, although our analyses across hundreds of model checkpoints and diverse benchmarks reveal a strong and statistically meaningful correlation between the proposed Alignment score and down-stream performance, this relationship is not universal across all evaluation settings. As shown in Fig. A8, a small number of benchmarks exhibit weak or negligible correlation, suggesting that factors beyond modality–text alignment may dominate performance in certain tasks. For example, some benchmarks may depend more heavily on modality-specific perception, reasoning format, dataset bias, or evaluation protocol than on the alignment properties captured by our score. Therefore, our Alignment score should be interpreted as a useful and broadly predictive indicator for cross-modal transfer, rather than as a complete explanation of all performance variations. Understanding when alignment is insufficient, and identifying complementary factors that better explain such exceptional cases, is an important direction for future work.
