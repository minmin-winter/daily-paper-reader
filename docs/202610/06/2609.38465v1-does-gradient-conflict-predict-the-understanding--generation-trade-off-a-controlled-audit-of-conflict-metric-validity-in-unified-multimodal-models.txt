# ABSTRACT 

Unified multimodal models (UMMs) are increasingly designed and optimized around the notion of gradient conflict between the understanding and genera-tion objectives, measured as cosine similarities between task gradients or conflict rates across layers. The premise that reducing these metrics improves the down-stream understanding–generation trade-off has never been tested directly. We au-dit it in a controlled testbed, G RID UMM, which mirrors the structural ingredients of UMM training—a fully shared trunk serving two objectives with asymmet-ric token budgets and task difficulty—while making the ground-truth trade-off exactly computable. Across 63 configurations (7 gradient-combination strate-gies × 3 data-mixing ratios × 3 seeds) and 372 measured checkpoints, no direc-tional conflict metric—global or per-layer cosine, conflict rate, removed energy— reaches |ρ| ≥ 0.3 with a confidence interval excluding zero for conflict mea-sured during training against the eventual trade-off (strongest |ρ| = 0 .145 , in-terval crossing zero), and the larger concurrent configuration-level associations are mutually sign-inconsistent. A dose–response intervention that monotonically suppresses conflict (level α) leaves the trade-off flat, separating correlation from causation. The one gradient-geometric quantity with association, the norm ratio, is a generation-failure detector, null among configurations that master generation. The functional interference measure eff rank leads every directional conflict met-ric ( ∆ρ CI 0.729–1.041) and retains its advantage inside that regime. Training loss, the outcome most often reported in place of benchmarks, tracks the trade-off strongly—the failure is specific to gradient-conflict geometry. Our results do not show that conflict is useless; they show that its validity as a diagnostic target must be established, not assumed, and we release the audit protocol as a reusable standard.

Uni-X (Hao et al., 2026) separates conflicting shallow and deep layers architecturally; Symbiotic-MoE (Liu et al., 2026) shields conflict-driven forgetting with expert disentanglement; ML-FOP-SOAP (Lu & Armour, 2026) suppresses modality conflicts with Fisher-orthogonal projection and second-order preconditioning; Pareto LoRA (Wei et al., 2026) re-balances modality gradients by Pareto-optimal integration. Across this literature one premise recurs untested: that lowering a gradient-conflict metric moves the model toward a better understanding– generation trade-off .The premise deserves scrutiny because its two ends are measured on different scales: conflict metrics describe gradient geometry on a fixed probe batch, while the trade-off is a property of two bench-mark families evaluated after training under an evaluation practice that treats the two objectives independently (Wang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025). Existing evidence is consistent with decoupling: across seven training strategies and two post-hoc methods on Janus-Pro, none improved generation CLIPScore ( p > 0.5) even though all modify conflict (Rao & Rachuri, 2026); Lu & Armour (2026) report training-loss gains without downstream evaluation; and high understanding and high genera-tion scores do not imply alignment (Wang et al., 2026). But no study has measured the quantity that would settle the premise—the correlation between conflict metrics and trade-off position across a controlled population of training runs—because on production-scale UMMs this requires dozens of full training runs. This paper performs that audit in a controlled testbed, organized around three design requirements. First, the ground-truth trade-off must be computable exactly, ruling out benchmark noise from dom-inating a correlation analysis: we construct G RID UMM, a synthetic environment whose two tasks have exactly measurable accuracies and whose Pareto frontier over configurations can be computed without approximation (cf. Deb et al., 2002), while reproducing the structural ingredients of UMM training—a fully shared trunk, two asymmetric objectives, an order-of-magnitude token-budget asymmetry, and autoregressive generation against single-token classification—so that the conflict phenomenology matches what has been reported on production UMMs (Rao & Rachuri, 2026). Second, the audit must include an intervention, not only an observational correlation: we suppress conflict by projecting the generative gradient with controlled strength α, moving the conflict level monotonically while holding data, schedule, and parameters fixed. Third, the audit must be honest about measurement noise: we quantify the test–retest reliability of every downstream metric and report the ceiling that any correlation cannot exceed (Spearman, 1904; Shrout & Fleiss, 1979). Our contributions are as follows. • An audit framework combining observational correlations with bootstrap intervals, par-tial correlations controlling for training progress, reliability ceilings, and a causal dose– response intervention; the field’s implicit premise becomes four falsifiable hypotheses, each with a killing experiment. • A controlled testbed , G RID UMM, reproducing the gradient-conflict phenomenology re-ported on production UMMs at a compute scale that permits 63 configurations; we release the world, the protocol, and all measurement code. • The audit result : the directional conflict metrics—global and per-layer cosines, conflict rate, removed energy—are weak, fragile predictors of the trade-off, and suppressing con-flict does not improve it; the norm ratio, the one gradient-geometric quantity with associ-ation, is a generation-failure detector. Training loss tracks the trade-off strongly, locating the failure in gradient-conflict geometry specifically, while functional interference mea-sures predict it better than every conflict metric and retain that advantage in the regime that masters generation. • A diagnostic standard : a protocol any UMM practitioner can run to decide whether con-flict reduction is a valid intermediate target in their setting, and what to measure instead. A controlled testbed cannot establish that any particular production-scale conflict-metric claim is false; it establishes what a validity claim must demonstrate, shows that the demonstration is absent from the literature, and makes such a demonstration routine. # 2 RELATED WORK 

Unified architectures. A first family of UMMs trains one transformer over interleaved discrete tokens for both objectives: Show-o unifies autoregressive and discrete-diffusion modeling (Xie et al., 2025a), Emu3 demonstrates pure next-token prediction over interleaved tokens (Wang et al., 2024), and Janus, Janus-Pro, Chameleon, and Transfusion establish decoupled visual encoding, early-fusion token mixing, and combined language-modeling plus diffusion objectives (Wu et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Team, 2025; Zhou et al., 2025); later systems add progressive vocabu-laries, continuous tokens, visual autoregression, instruction tuning, and native unified generation (Tang et al., 2025; Fan et al., 2025; Zhuang et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025b; Tong et al., 2025; Team, 2026; Xu et al., 2025) (cf. Zhao et al., 2026). Our testbed abstracts the shared structure common to these designs—one trunk, one parameter space, two objectives with unequal token budgets (Ap-pendix A)—while replacing natural data with a grammar whose ground-truth trade-off is exactly computable. 

Conflict-motivated UMM design and diagnosis without validation. A family of 2025–2026 works takes gradient conflict between the two objectives as the design driver: Uni-X separates con-flicting shallow and deep layers (Hao et al., 2026), Symbiotic-MoE shields conflict-driven forgetting (Liu et al., 2026), ML-FOP-SOAP suppresses variance-induced conflicts with Fisher-orthogonal projection and SOAP-style preconditioning (Lu & Armour, 2026; Vyas et al., 2025), Pareto LoRA integrates imbalanced modality gradients (Wei et al., 2026), and task-aware MoE routing resolves task-objective conflicts (Zhang & Tang, 2026). For each, the conflict metric is a premise and the trade-off is reported separately. Closest to questioning the premise, Rao & Rachuri (2026) measure near-orthogonal gradients and find no DPO variant improves generation CLIPScore, and cross-task consistency benchmarks (Wang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025) show that the two scores do not imply alignment; synergy, adversary, and continual-training studies characterize the phenomenon or miti-gate its symptoms (Wu et al., 2026; Su et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2025a). None validates the diagnostic metric itself. 

Multi-task optimization. The optimizer-side interventions borrowed by UMM work originate in multi-task learning (Caruana, 1997; Vandenhende et al., 2021), with shared architectures from routing (Ma et al., 2018) to task-interaction networks (Vandenhende et al., 2020), loss weights adapted by uncertainty (Kendall et al., 2018) or auxiliary-gradient magnitudes (He et al., 2022), and gradient manipulation reshaping geometry directly: minimum-norm combinations (Sener & Koltun, 2018), gradient surgery and conflict-averse updates (see Vandenhende et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2021), bargaining-game solutions (Navon et al., 2022), independent-component alignment (Senushkin et al., 2023), with Pareto concepts from multi-objective optimization (Deb et al., 2002). Our audit uses faithful implementations of these methods as the population over which conflict met-rics vary—if conflict metrics fail to predict trade-offs even across methods whose entire purpose is to reshape gradient geometry, the metric’s diagnostic value is in doubt. 

Modality imbalance. Joint multimodal training is known to favor the faster-learning modality: the greedy-dynamics analysis of Wang et al. (2020) explains why multimodal networks can under-perform unimodal ones, and balancing methods modulate gradients or prototypes on the fly (Peng et al., 2022; Fan et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2025b; Yang et al., 2025; 2026). Our norm-ratio finding connects to this literature: magnitude imbalance is real and diagnostic, but of generation failure, not of a directional conflict signal (Section 4.2). 

Audits of benchmark validity. Our protocol follows the tradition of audits that re-examine whether an accepted evaluation practice measures what it claims: rebuilt test sets revealed overfit-ting to reused ImageNet splits (Recht et al., 2019; Vasudevan et al., 2022), controlled re-evaluation collapsed metric-learning gains (Musgrave et al., 2020), and methodological reviews document the cost of unvalidated proxies (Varoquaux & Cheplygina, 2022). To our knowledge, no analogous audit exists for training-time conflict metrics in UMMs; this paper supplies one. Appendix A discusses each of these five literatures in more detail. 3Preprint

# 3 A C ONTROLLED TESTBED FOR CONFLICT -M ETRIC VALIDITY 

3.1 WHY A CONTROLLED TESTBED 

Auditing the validity of a diagnostic metric requires varying the metric while holding everything else fixed, measuring the ground-truth target without noise, and intervening on the metric causally. On a production UMM the correlation analysis alone would require ≥63 full training runs per design point, and the trade-off is observed through benchmarks whose seed-to-seed variance can exceed the differences being measured (Section 4.7). A controlled environment solves both problems at once and makes the audit pre-registerable; the price is external validity, managed by matching the structural ingredients of UMM training and verifying that the testbed’s conflict phenomenology reproduces production measurements (Section 4). GRID UMM places two objectives over one decoder-only transformer. The data world is a 5 × 5 grid over eight colors; a scene contains 6–8 non-overlapping rectangular objects, and both tasks share a single interleaved marker/color token representation whose learned position markers anchor each output slot to its coordinate. Generation maps a description—objects enumerated in randomized order—to the marker/color stream autoregressively (25 color tokens plus end-of-stream, against 33– 61 description tokens); understanding answers a question about the same stream (cell color, color count, or color-pair adjacency) with a single-token answer. Held-out sets are exact samples of the same grammar under fixed seeds, so downstream accuracies are unbiased estimates of generaliza-tion. Two regime facts matter for reading the audit: generation is mastered well before the budget ends by every configuration that trains its gradient at natural scale—the one weighting scheme that scales it down by the token-count ratio (token-normalized) fails to master it in a subset of seeds, an imbalance pathology visible in Table 1—so most trade-off variation lives on the understanding axis; and the conflict phenomenology’s magnitude is concentrated in the learning phase, exactly where the field’s premise operates (Figure 6, Appendix E). Every parameter is shared by both tasks: the strongest form of the UMM design point and the one under which conflict claims are made. 4Preprint 3.2 TRAINING PROTOCOL 

All runs use a two-backward protocol : each step draws a batch of 64 samples with the configu-ration’s data-mixing ratio, computes Lu and Lg in separate forward/backward passes, and captures per-group gradients (embedding, each of 6 blocks, and the output head) per objective. The update direction is the combination produced by the configuration’s method (Appendix C); conflict statis-tics are measured on a fixed probe batch (32+32 samples, identical across all runs) every 50 steps, so cross-configuration comparisons are not confounded by batch composition. Because the generation objective is autoregressive, we train it with a teacher/student-forcing mix-ture : before step 200 the loss is pure teacher forcing (Williams & Zipser, 1989) on the ground-truth prefix; the student-forced share then ramps linearly to 0.400, i.e., for that share of samples the loss is computed on the model’s own sampled prefix while the targets remain ground truth. The mix-ture keeps the generation gradient alive throughout training; Appendix D documents the schedule. Training runs 900 steps with AdamW ( 1×10 −3, warmup 100, cosine decay), batch 64, and the understanding-to-generation data ratio drawn from {1:2 , 1:1 , 2:1 }.3.3 CONFLICT METRICS

To compare configurations on a single axis we define the

A configuration is Pareto dominated by another if the latter matches or exceeds it on both axes with at least one strict inequality. We avoid min–max normalization: with a saturating generation axis it would amplify resampling noise into the ranking (Appendix D). 3.5 FUNCTIONAL INTERFERENCE MEASURES 

Because our hypotheses contrast parameter-space gradient geometry with functional interference, we measure four candidates at each checkpoint, drawing on the probing and representation-similarity literature (Belinkov, 2022; Klabunde et al., 2025). (i) Probe separability : accuracy of a linear probe on the trunk’s mean-pooled last-block features predicting the answer token of held-out questions (2-fold cross-validated). (ii) Subspace overlap : mean principal-angle cosine between the top-8 singular subspaces of the two tasks’ last-block feature matrices. (iii) Effective rank : participation ratio of the covariance spectrum of the pooled feature matrix. (iv) Self-consistency : the fraction of questions about the model’s own generated scenes that it answers correctly—a functional analogue of the cross-task consistency XTC-Bench measures at the benchmark level (Wang et al., 2026). 3.6 HYPOTHESES

# 4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 SETUP 

The population consists of 63 base-scale configurations—7 gradient-combination strategies × 3data ratios (1:2, 1:1, 2:1) × 3 seeds—plus 12 intervention configurations (E3) and 18 configurations at a second scale (E5): 93 training runs, 372 measured checkpoints, each with the full conflict-metric and downstream vectors (Table 4, Appendix E). The compute budget was a single Apple M4 Max laptop (MPS, float32), up to six concurrent configurations, 18.8 aggregate run-hours; every manifest records device, software, and wall-clock time. Methods are faithful implementations of the published algorithms (Appendix C); two documented simplifications are noted there. 4.2 E1: DOES CONFLICT PREDICT THE TRADE -OFF ?The operative premise is predictive: practitioners measure conflict during training and read it as a signal about the eventual trade-off. We test both readings. The predictive analysis correlates conflict statistics measured at 25% of the budget with the final Pareto score ( N = 63 ); the con-current analysis correlates conflict and trade-off at the same checkpoint across all 252 rows with cluster-bootstrap intervals, and a partial Spearman correlation removes the training-progress compo-nent (rank-regressing out the step index), addressing the trivial concern that both quantities simply improve with training. Figure 2 shows the core result: conflict magnitude measured during the learning phase against the final trade-off, every configuration plotted. The rank correlation is ρ = −0.198 (95% CI [-0.463, 0.079]). Table 5 reports the full conflict-metric vector at both levels (Appendix E): at the predic-tive level—the field’s operative reading—no directional conflict metric reaches |ρ| ≥ 0.3 with a confidence interval excluding zero. and hence monotonically suppressing conflict while holding data, schedule, and parameters fixed. A correlation cannot exceed the reliability of its measures (Spearman, 1904), and an audit that ig-nores this can mistake measurement noise for a null result. We resample the held-out sets five times per final checkpoint and compute ICC(1,1) (Shrout & Fleiss, 1979): 0.982 for understanding accu-racy, 1.000 for generation object-cell accuracy. The geometric-mean reliability implies a correlation ceiling of prel u · rel g = 0 .991 ; disattenuating the observed config-level correlation gives -0.324— the association remains weak after crediting the metrics for all attenuation benchmark noise can explain. On power: with N = 63 configurations the minimum detectable |ρ| at α=0 .05 , power 0.8

is 0.362; the checkpoint level ( N = 252 ) reaches 0.178. The audit is therefore powered to detect moderate associations and the reported CIs are informative, not vacuous. 

# 5 DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 

What the audit does and does not show. The result is a statement about metric validity, not about the usefulness of any intervention. In the controlled regime we can establish the premise’s 8Preprint operational content—that a conflict metric measured during training carries information about the eventual trade-off—and find it unsupported for the directional metrics the field keys on: the associ-ation is weak at the predictive level (largest |ρ| = 0 .14 , interval crossing zero) and unstable where it is larger (the concurrent configuration-level associations are sign-inconsistent and dissolve under the regime cut), and it does not survive the reliability ceiling correction, the progress control, or a per-layer reweighting. The causal intervention shows that even removing conflict does not move the trade-off, ruling out the strongest rescue (”the correlation is diluted but causally real”). None of this contradicts the possibility that on production-scale UMMs conflict metrics behave differently; it establishes that the burden of demonstration sits with the claimant, and provides the protocol that makes the demonstration routine rather than an act of faith. 

Why the field adopted an unvalidated proxy. Conflict metrics are cheap, continuous, and mea-surable every step, whereas the trade-off requires benchmark suites with known reliability problems (Recht et al., 2019; Musgrave et al., 2020; Varoquaux & Cheplygina, 2022), and the proxy has an intuitive mechanism story. Our results do not make the proxy useless; they show that its validity is setting-dependent , and that the settings where it was adopted never checked it. The protocol of Section 3.6 costs a fraction of the intervention methods’ compute and returns an explicit go/no-go signal. 

The method population exhibits the pathologies the field fears. The method rows are them-selves informative. Under an order-of-magnitude task-norm imbalance, MGDA’s minimum-norm combination collapses onto the minority-gradient task: understanding stalls at 0.389 while gener-ation reaches 1.000—a pathology mirroring, in miniature, the modality-imbalance complaints that motivated balancing methods (Wei et al., 2026; Peng et al., 2022; Fan et al., 2023). SOAP-style pre-conditioning collapses the generation axis outright (object-cell accuracy 0.000, joint score 0.271 in all nine runs)—and its conflict statistics sit inside the healthy range (cosine 0.002, rate 0.389, versus naive training’s 0.001 and 0.417): the worst method is invisible to every directional conflict metric, and only the norm ratio flags it, for the failure-detection reason established in E1. Method choice changes where a configuration lands on the two axes (Table 1) without any conflict metric tracking those differences—precisely the decoupling the audit is designed to expose. 

Functional measures won—carefully interpreted. The best functional predictor (eff rank, ρ =0.702 ) outperformed every directional conflict metric by a clear paired-bootstrap margin and retains its advantage in the regime that masters generation (self consistency, ρ = 0 .854 ). The strongest gradient-geometric competitor, the norm ratio ( ρ = −0.651 ), is a generation-failure detector, null among configurations that master generation. The functional measures stay strong exactly where the field would deploy them. We read this as evidence for the construct the field was reaching for: interference between the tasks’ computations , manifesting in representation geometry and cross-task functional consistency rather than parameter-space gradient directions. We do not claim these measures are the answer; they are the leading candidates in a competition that, to our knowledge, had never been run. 

Limitations. (i) Scale : the testbed is 3.2M–6.7M parameters on a synthetic grammar; the audit’s logic and protocol transfer to production scales, its quantitative conclusions do not automatically; we mitigate by reproducing the conflict phenomenology and releasing a protocol whose cost scales linearly in the number of configurations. (ii) Regime : generation is mastered by every configu-ration training its gradient at natural scale (the token-normalized weighting collapses it in a sub-set of seeds), so trade-off variation mostly lives on the understanding axis; the audit’s machinery is unchanged when both axes vary. (iii) Method coverage : seven gradient-combination strategies spanning the families used by the examined literature (Vandenhende et al., 2021); architecture and data-scheduling interventions are not swept. (iv) Power : with 63 configurations the minimum de-tectable correlation is 0.362; the CIs are the honest statement of what the design can and cannot rule out, and the checkpoint-level analysis with 252 rows tightens detection to 0.178. 

# 6 CONCLUSION 

A family of UMM designs and optimizers is built on an assumption that has never been tested: that lowering a gradient-conflict metric improves the understanding–generation trade-off. We turned that 9Preprint assumption into four falsifiable hypotheses, built a controlled testbed in which each has a designated experiment, ran the audit across 93 training configurations with reliability ceilings and a causal intervention, and found the assumption unsupported: directional conflict metrics carry no more than weak and statistically fragile signal about the trade-off, suppressing conflict causally does not improve it, and functional interference measures predict it better—while training loss, in the same testbed, tracks the trade-off strongly, locating the failure in gradient-conflict geometry specifically. 10 Preprint AI USE STATEMENT

# A EXTENDED RELATED WORK 

Unified architectures, in detail. Show-o (Xie et al., 2025a) unifies autoregressive and discrete-diffusion modeling in one transformer; Emu3 (Wang et al., 2024) demonstrates pure next-token pre-diction over interleaved visual and textual tokens; Janus (Wu et al., 2025) separates understanding-and generation-oriented visual encoders while unifying the trunk, and Janus-Pro (Chen et al., 2025) scales that recipe with data and parameter scaling. Chameleon (Team, 2025) establishes early-fusion token-based mixed-modal training with a stability recipe for image-text interleaves; Trans-fusion (Zhou et al., 2025) combines a language-modeling objective over text with a diffusion ob-jective over image patches in one model; Unified-IO 2 (Lu et al., 2024) extends autoregressive multimodality across vision, language, audio, and action. Later unified systems pursue progressive vocabulary learning (Tang et al., 2025), continuous-token autoregression (Fan et al., 2025), visual-autoregressive generation inside an MLLM (Zhuang et al., 2025), improved native unified modeling (Xie et al., 2025b), visual-predictive instruction tuning (Tong et al., 2025), native unified image generation at scale (Team, 2026), and decoupled autoregressive understanding-generation founda-tion models (Xu et al., 2025); surveys consolidate this rapidly growing family (Zhao et al., 2026). Across all of these designs, one trunk (or one tied trunk family) serves objectives with very different token budgets and output structures; our testbed isolates exactly that shared structure. 

Conflict-motivated UMM design, in detail. Uni-X (Hao et al., 2026) reports that gradient con-flicts between vision and text concentrate in shallow and deep layers of a shared autoregressive transformer and diminish in middle layers, and proposes a two-end-separated, middle-shared archi-tecture; its evidence for downstream benefit is training efficiency and benchmark scores, not a mea-sured link between conflict reduction and trade-off improvement. Symbiotic-MoE (Liu et al., 2026) 15 Preprint frames understanding-side catastrophic forgetting as the consequence of severe gradient conflicts and introduces modality-aware expert disentanglement with early-stage gradient shielding. ML-FOP-SOAP (Lu & Armour, 2026) shows that first-order optimizers are vulnerable to cross-modality gradient heterogeneity and proposes Fisher-orthogonal projection with SOAP-style preconditioning (Vyas et al., 2025); its reported evidence is training-loss sample efficiency and wall-clock speed on Janus and Emu3, without downstream benchmark evaluation. Pareto LoRA (Wei et al., 2026) measures order-of-magnitude modality-specific gradient imbalance under LoRA fine-tuning and in-tegrates text- and image-side gradients by Pareto optimality. Task-aware MoE routing (Zhang & Tang, 2026) resolves task-objective conflicts by routing. 

Diagnosis without validation, in detail. Rao & Rachuri (2026) perform a gradient analysis of DPO on Janus-Pro, finding near-orthogonal understanding–generation gradients with 11–14 × mag-nitude imbalance driven by visual token count asymmetry, and report that no DPO variant improves generation CLIPScore ( p > 0.5); their analysis is confined to preference tuning on a single ar-chitecture and does not measure the conflict–trade-off correlation across a controlled population. XTC-Bench (Wang et al., 2026) demonstrates that existing evaluation treats understanding and gen-eration independently and that high scores on both do not imply cross-task consistency; UniEval similarly argues for holistic evaluation of unified models (Li et al., 2025). Wu et al. (2026) study when the two objectives reinforce or compete in controlled native settings and find one objective tends to dominate when both are forced through the same computation path; Su et al. (2026) exploit the understanding–generation inconsistency for post-training; Wei et al. (2025a) study intra- and inter-modal forgetting in continual UMM training. These works characterize the phenomenon or mitigate its downstream symptoms; none validates the diagnostic metric itself. 

Multi-task optimization, in detail. Joint training of multiple tasks predates deep multimodal models (Caruana, 1997) and is surveyed for dense prediction (Vandenhende et al., 2021). Archi-tectural sharing ranges from multi-gate mixture of experts (Ma et al., 2018) to multi-scale task-interaction networks (Vandenhende et al., 2020). On the optimization side, uncertainty-based loss weighting (Kendall et al., 2018) and auxiliary-gradient-magnitude balancing (He et al., 2022) adapt scalar weights; gradient manipulation rescales or projects the vectors themselves: minimum-norm convex combinations (Sener & Koltun, 2018), projection-based gradient surgery and conflict-averse cone constraints (see Vandenhende et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2021), bargaining-game Nash solutions (Navon et al., 2022), and independent-component alignment that upweights conflict-prone gradi-ent components (Senushkin et al., 2023). Pareto-dominance concepts enter multi-task evaluation from multi-objective optimization (Deb et al., 2002). Our method population spans these families at faithful implementations, so that conflict metrics vary across methods designed precisely to reshape conflict. 

Modality imbalance, in detail. Wang et al. (2020) show that end-to-end multimodal training can underperform unimodal training because the gradient is dominated by the faster-learning modal-ity, and propose gradient modulation to rebalance it; their on-the-fly modulation line continues with generalized-median balancing (Peng et al., 2022) and intra-network modulation (Wei et al., 2025b). Prototypical rebalancing addresses the imbalance at the decision level (Fan et al., 2023), instantaneous probe-and-rebalance methods monitor and correct imbalance during training (Yang et al., 2025), and modality-laziness analyses document when the model ignores one modality en-tirely (Yang et al., 2026). These works establish that magnitude imbalance between task gradients is real and consequential—the premise our norm-ratio finding refines: in our testbed the imbalance statistic is diagnostic of generation failure rather than of directional conflict, and is null exactly where the field would deploy it. 

Audits of benchmark validity, in detail. Recht et al. (Recht et al., 2019) rebuilt ImageNet and CIFAR-10 test sets and showed that classifier accuracy drops substantially, revealing overfitting to reused evaluation data; follow-up work characterized the remaining mistakes and their distribu-tion (Vasudevan et al., 2022). In metric learning, Musgrave et al. (2020) re-evaluated four years of claimed improvements under a controlled protocol and found the ranking of methods largely unchanged. Methodological reviews in medical imaging trace systematic failures to unvalidated proxies and evaluation practices (Varoquaux & Cheplygina, 2022). Our audit imports this discipline into UMM training diagnostics: the target quantity (the trade-off) is measured without benchmark 16 Preprint idiosyncrasy, the predictor family (conflict metrics) is fixed before analysis, and the conclusion is scoped by explicit reliability and power statements.

# D PROTOCOL DETAILS 

Teacher/student-forcing mixture. Before step 200 the generation loss is pure teacher forcing on the ground-truth color tokens at the marker anchors; the student-forced share then ramps linearly to 0.400 by step 500 and stays there (capped at 16 of the batch’s generation samples per step). Student-forced rows render colors by sampling from the model at temperature 1.0 (masked to the color alphabet) at the true marker anchors, while the targets remain the ground-truth colors; the rendering loss is computed at the marker positions and the end-of-stream position (Section 3). Probe snapshots use the same mixture with a run-independent generator seed per snapshot, making conflict statistics comparable across configurations. 

Measurement alignment. The audit required three correctness invariants, each covered by a unit check in the released code ( selfcheck.py ): (i) the causal mask in every attention path (full-sequence forwards and KV-cached prefill), verified by exact equality (float64) of cached and full-forward greedy decoding over equal-length batches, the calling convention used by evaluation; (ii) the grid-value-to-color convention, verified by a 500-scene round trip from scene integers to tokens and back; (iii) loss alignment, verified by asserting that the batched rendering loss repro-duces a brute-force per-row computation in which color gj is read at the position of marker ⟨pj ⟩

and the end-of-stream target at the last color-token position, for rows of differing description lengths inside one padded batch. 

Pareto score and frontier. The score is the mean of the two raw accuracies, S = (acc u + acc g )/2;the empirical Pareto frontier is computed on the raw two-dimensional accuracy plane over all E1 configurations at the final checkpoint, and a configuration is Pareto-dominated by another if the latter matches or exceeds it on both axes with one strict inequality (Deb et al., 2002). We deliberately avoid min–max normalization: with a saturating generation axis it would amplify resample noise into the score. 

Statistics. Spearman rank correlations (Spearman, 1904); percentile bootstrap confidence inter-vals with B=5000 (Efron, 1979) (pairs resampled at the configuration level, clusters resampled at the checkpoint level so that all checkpoints of a run move together); exact p-values from the permutation-free normal approximation of the rank correlation under the null; partial Spearman by rank-regressing out the step index.
