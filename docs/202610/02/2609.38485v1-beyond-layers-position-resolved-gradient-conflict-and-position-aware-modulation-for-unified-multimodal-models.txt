## ABSTRACT 

Unified multimodal models (UMMs) train image understanding and autoregres-sive image generation on shared parameters, and the two objectives are known to interfere. Existing diagnoses and remedies operate at the resolution of layers or experts: conflict is measured per layer and resolved by separating parameters through two-end architectures, task-aware mixtures of experts, or gradient surgery. We argue that this resolution hides an orthogonal axis. Generation in a UMM is next-token prediction over a raster sequence of visual tokens whose roles vary systematically with position—early tokens fix global layout and low-frequency structure, late tokens fill in texture—so how strongly a generation gradient inter-feres with understanding should depend on where in the sequence it originates. We introduce a position-resolved interference map that attributes the understanding– generation gradient conflict to visual-token positions within every layer, computed from a single backward pass with position-grouped gradient hooks at 1.2× the cost of a standard backward pass. On Show-o and Janus-Pro, position explains a substantial share of conflict variance after controlling for depth (partial η2 = 0 .31 

vs. 0.35 for layer on Show-o; 0.15 vs. 0.30 on Janus-Pro, whose understanding branch bypasses the visual-token sequence): the first quarter of the sequence has a mean gradient cosine of −0.18 against understanding, the last quarter −0.02 . The dependence survives per-position gradient-norm normalization, retaining 80% of its effect size, so it is directional rather than a magnitude artifact, and conflict strength tracks the semantic content of a position (Spearman ρ = 0 .64 ). Build-ing on the map, we propose position-aware modulation (P AM ), which removes the anti-aligned component of generation gradients only at high-conflict positions without changing the architecture. Under a matched trainable-parameter budget PAM improves over layer-wise separation by +21 MME and +2 .4 GenEval points while matching it on POPE and overall FID, whereas a random-position control with the same number of modulated tokens recovers only about 31% of the gain; the cost is a 4% relative degradation of the high-frequency component of FID, with overall FID unchanged. Position-based and layer-based separation are com-plementary degrees of freedom and can be combined.

Training such a model on both objectives is known to be harder than training either alone: recent diagnostic and architectural work reports gradient- and representation-level conflict between the two objectives and resolves it by separating parameters —along depth (Hao et al., 2026), across experts (Liu et al., 2026; Zhang & Tang, 2026), through second-order corrections (Lu & Armour, 2026; Vyas et al., 2025), or by Pareto-optimal gradient integration (Wei et al., 2026). All of these diagnoses share one measurement resolution: they ask which layers or which experts 

conflict, and they answer with parameter-space separations. This paper changes the unit of measure-ment. A UMM generates an image as next-token prediction over a raster sequence of visual tokens, and the roles of those tokens vary systematically with position: early tokens determine global layout and low-frequency structure, late tokens fill in texture and high-frequency detail. Tokenizer research arrives at the same conclusion from the representation side—semantic abstraction and pixel recon-struction place different demands on a token (Song et al., 2026; Guo et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026)—and token-level gradient imbalance has been observed inside multimodal latent reasoning (Han et al., 2026). If the content of a visual token depends on its position, the conflict 

that a generation gradient makes with understanding should depend on position too. No existing measurement can see this: a layer-level conflict score averages over the entire sequence and discards exactly the axis we study. We introduce the position-resolved interference map : for every layer ℓ and position bucket b of the visual-token sequence, the cosine between the generation gradient attributed to bucket b and the understanding gradient. (ii) The position dependence is directional. Gradient norms decline by 2.6× from the first to the last bucket, so a skeptic could attribute the map to magnitude imbalance (Han et al., 2026); after per-position normalization the position effect retains 80% of its size, and conflict strength tracks semantic decodability of a position (Spearman ρ = 0 .64 , n = 96 ). (iii) The map transfers to a method. The position effect halves on Janus-Pro, whose understanding branch bypasses the visual-token sequence, with a random control recovering 60% of a smaller gain: the axis is strong where the objectives share the token sequence and weak where they do not (Section 8).

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Understanding–generation conflict in UMMs. Diagnostic work establishes that the objectives interfere at the gradient and representation level (Rao & Rachuri, 2026; Wu et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Su et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2025), and the remedies are all parameter-space separations: two-2Preprint end-separated architectures (Hao et al., 2026), task-aware experts (Zhang & Tang, 2026; Liu et al., 2026), second-order corrections (Lu & Armour, 2026), and Pareto-optimal LoRA integration (Wei et al., 2026). They differ in where parameters are separated and share the measurement unit of a parameter block. Our map keeps parameters shared and changes the unit to the token position; the two axes are orthogonal and composable (Section 7). 

Multi-task gradient conflict. Gradient surgery treats tasks as atomic scalar objectives and mod-ulates their gradients globally (Yu et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021; Sener & Koltun, 2018; Chen et al., 2018; Navon et al., 2022). Per-token gradient heterogeneity has been observed in multimodal la-tent reasoning, where visual tokens receive systematically smaller gradient norms than text tokens (Han et al., 2026), and critical-token selection drives recent RLVR results for autoregressive image generation (Zhang et al., 2026). Neither line connects token identity to understanding–generation conflict; our map is that connection, and P AM is the modulation it licenses. 

Semantic vs. reconstruction tokenization. A parallel line redesigns the tokenizer to separate semantic and reconstruction information (Song et al., 2026; Guo et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026). Those works change the representation ; we keep the tokenizer fixed and change the 

optimization of gradients flowing through it. Their existence is evidence that token content varies systematically—the premise our map measures directly. Appendix A extends this discussion. 

## 3 SETUP : MEASURING CONFLICT AT TOKEN RESOLUTION 

Model and notation. A UMM shares parameters θ between an understanding objective Lu (text-conditioned image or text prediction) and a generation objective Lg (autoregressive prediction over a raster sequence of T visual tokens produced by a VQ tokenizer (van den Oord et al., 2017; Esser et al., 2021)). We index layers ℓ ∈ { 1, . . . , L } and split the raster sequence into K = 16 contiguous 

position buckets b ∈ { 1, . . . , K } along the raster order: bucket 1 holds the first T /K tokens (which determine global layout), bucket K the last (texture). We study two architectures with different relationships between the objectives and the visual-token stream: Show-o-1.3B (Xie et al., 2025a) (L = 24 , d=2048 , T =256 from a 16 ×16 raster), whose understanding pathway consumes the same VQ vocabulary, and Janus-Pro-7B (Chen et al., 2025) ( L = 30 , d=4096 , T =576 from a 24 ×24 

raster), whose understanding pathway runs on a CLIP-style SigLIP encoder (Radford et al., 2021; Zhai et al., 2023) and never touches the visual-token sequence.

## 5 THE MAP IS DIRECTIONAL , AND IT IS ABOUT SEMANTICS 

Norm or direction? Token gradients are known to be norm-heterogeneous (Han et al., 2026), and in our diagnostic batches the generation-gradient norm declines monotonically by 2.63 × from the first to the last bucket on Show-o ( 1.92 × on Janus-Pro; Fig. 2b). A skeptic can therefore re-read Figure 1 as a magnitude artifact: early tokens simply move shared parameters more. We test this by re-computing the map with per-position normalized gradients. The position effect survives: partial 

η2 drops from 0.312 to 0.249 on Show-o and from 0.150 to 0.114 on Janus-Pro—a retention of 

79 .7% and 75 .8% (Fig. 2c, Table 2). Under the pre-registered decision rule (retention below 50% 

would have falsified the direction claim and reduced the direction to a normalization trick), the di-rectional interpretation stands: early generation gradients point against the understanding optimum, late ones do not, and this is not explained by their magnitudes. 

What carries the conflict? The pre-registered hypothesis was that conflict concentrates on seman-tically loaded positions—early raster tokens, which decide layout—because those positions com-pete with understanding for the same semantic representation. We measure semantic load directly rather than assuming it: a linear probe (Alain & Bengio, 2017) decodes a DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024) global embedding from each bucket’s token embeddings, giving a decodability sb ∈ [0 , 1] 

(R2 on 10k held-out images); and a per-bucket high-frequency reconstruction residual rb measures how texture-heavy a position is. ## 8 DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 

What the resolution buys. Prior measurements answer “which layers fight”; the map answers “which layers fight over which part of the image ”, licensing modulations that keep parameters shared—and the combination arm is the best model on both axes in our study. 

Where the axis weakens. Janus-Pro decouples understanding from the visual-token stream, and the position effect halves ( η2pos = 0 .15 vs. 0.31 ) with the random control recovering a majority of a smaller gain. The position axis is a property of architectures where the objectives share the token sequence ; η2pos read from the map is a decision variable for whether P AM is worth running. 

Limitations. (1) The map is a first-order measurement of a fixed diagnostic set; representation-level (Wu et al., 2026) and second-order (Lu & Armour, 2026) views are complementary, not re-placed. (2) Position semantics are tokenizer- and resolution-dependent; the map is recomputed per model in 28 H200-hours and no position priors are claimed to transfer unmodified. (3) The tex-ture cost is measured in the frequency domain (Table 4); human evaluation is future work. (4) The diagnostic set is small (1,024 samples); replicate stability is high, but scale could sharpen fine layer-band boundaries. (5) Interactions of P AM with full fine-tuning or representation-level separations (Liu et al., 2026) are untested. 9Preprint

## A EXTENDED RELATED WORK 

Autoregressive visual generation and raster order. The premise that visual-token roles vary with position has a long history in autoregressive image modeling. Image GPT (Chen et al., 2020a) predicts pixels in raster order with a plain Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017); discrete tokenizers (van den Oord et al., 2017; Esser et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2024) made the same recipe scalable to high-resolution text-to-image generation (Ramesh et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2024), and masked-token generation (Chang et al., 2022) decodes tokens in parallel over a few iterations rather than in raster order. Two recent lines make position an explicit design variable: next-scale prediction (Tian et al., 2024) orders tokens from coarse to fine so that early steps fix global structure, and ran-domized autoregressive training (Yu et al., 2025) anneals from random permutations to raster order and shows that the factorization order materially affects sample quality. Rotary position encoding (Su et al., 2024) is the mechanism through which the backbones we study bind a token to its raster index. Our map measures a consequence of this positional structure that these works do not: how much the gradient from each position conflicts with a second objective sharing the same parameters. 

Unified models and unified tokenizers. Beyond the systems named in Section 1, unified models differ mainly in how the understanding pathway meets the visual-token stream. SEED-LLaMA (Ge et al., 2024) and VILA-U (Wu et al., 2025b) tokenize images into semantically aligned discrete codes so that a single next-token objective serves both tasks. TokenFlow (Qu et al., 2025) decouples semantic and pixel-level codebooks behind shared indices, whereas UniTok (Ma et al., 2025) argues that reconstruction and semantic supervision do not inherently conflict and enlarges the codebook instead. Show-o2 (Xie et al., 2025b) and BAGEL (Deng et al., 2025) route generation through continuous latents with flow matching; BAGEL uses a mixture-of-transformers backbone (Liang et al., 2025) whose modality-specific parameters are a form of the layer- and expert-level separation discussed in Section 2, and LMFusion (Shi et al., 2025) freezes the language modules of a pretrained LLM and trains parallel image modules, a separation motivated explicitly by forgetting of text-only capabilities. Understanding-only models trained by visual instruction tuning (Liu et al., 2023) are the reference point for the understanding axis of all of these systems. Because the position effect we measure is strongest when both objectives share the token sequence (Section 8), we expect it to be largest for the SEED and VILA-U family and smallest for designs that, like Janus-Pro, feed understanding through a separate encoder. 

Multi-task optimization and modality competition. Gradient-conflict remedies for generic multi-task learning include Nash-bargaining aggregation of task gradients (Navon et al., 2022), alignment targets that pull the updates of related tasks together (Wang et al., 2021), and stochastic sign masking of inconsistent gradient components (Chen et al., 2020b); the cosine between task gra-dients that our map evaluates per position was proposed as an on-line signal for weighting auxiliary losses by Du et al. (2018). Large-scale re-evaluations (Kurin et al., 2022; Xin et al., 2022) find that many specialized multi-task optimizers do not beat a well-tuned scalarization, which is one reason we compare P AM against a matched-budget parameter-separation arm and a random-position control rather than against generic gradient surgery alone. The multimodal-learning literature documents that jointly trained multimodal networks can underperform their unimodal counterparts because the modalities learn at different rates (Wang et al., 2020), and corrects the imbalance by modulating each modality’s gradient on the fly (Peng et al., 2022); P AM applies the same modulation idea at the finer resolution of token positions within one modality. Selecting which token positions to train on has also been shown to matter in language-model pretraining (Lin et al., 2024), which is consistent with our finding that the position structure, not the act of modulating tokens, carries the P AM effect. 17 Preprint 

Parameter-efficient adaptation and forgetting. All arms are trained with LoRA (Hu et al., 2022), which is known to forget less of the base model than full fine-tuning at the price of learning less (Biderman et al., 2024); the interaction of P AM with full fine-tuning is listed as an open question in Section 8. Optimization uses AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019), and the probe is implemented with torch.autograd hooks (Paszke et al., 2019). 

Frequency-domain evaluation and statistics. The band-wise FID of Table 4 decomposes images with a Laplacian pyramid (Burt & Adelson, 1983). Reporting the texture cost per frequency band follows the observation that neural networks fit low-frequency structure first (Rahaman et al., 2019), so an intervention that acts on early, low-frequency positions is expected to trade against the high-frequency band. Semantic decodability is measured with linear probes (Alain & Bengio, 2017); effect sizes are reported as partial η2 (Cohen, 1988) and confidence intervals by paired bootstrap (Efron & Tibshirani, 1993).

## C ANOVA AND BOOTSTRAP PROTOCOLS 

The ANOVA of Table 1 treats the map cells as observations of a balanced design: each (layer, bucket) cell is observed once per module-group (5 groups: attention q, k, v, o and MLP) and per batch-seed replicate (24), yielding L·K ·5·24 observations. Module and batch effects are additive and balanced, so the error mean square is their pooled residual; main-effect and interaction sums of squares follow the standard balanced two-way decomposition, and partial η2 is SS effect /(SS effect + SS err ).
