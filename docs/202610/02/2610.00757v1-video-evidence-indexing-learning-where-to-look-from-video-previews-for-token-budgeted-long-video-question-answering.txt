## ABSTRACT 

Long-video question answering is limited by the high cost of visual tokens and by the fixed context width of current VLMs. A long-video question may require broad temporal coverage, but the answer is often supported by only a compact set of moments. To locate these moments efficiently, we propose token-budgeted Video Evidence Indexing (VEI): given a dense low-resolution Video Preview, the model constructs a compact high-resolution Evidence Set for final reasoning. We treat VEI as a policy that must jointly solve evidence localization , which finds question-relevant moments, and budget planning , which decides where to spend the limited high-resolution frame budget. We implement this idea with an inference pipeline: the Video Preview provides cheap global coverage, Video Evidence Indexing constructs the Evidence Set, and Answer Generation combines both inputs for final VQA. To address missing frame-level supervision, we adopt privileged self-distillation, where an answer-aware teacher guides the normal test-time policy on student-generated indexing traces. We explore previews at 1, 6, 12, and 24 visual tokens per frame, training a single policy that supports all four resolutions. Experiments show that Video Evidence Indexing improves accuracy under limited visual budgets, and self-distillation further improves both QA accuracy and temporal evidence localization. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Recent advances in Video Language Models (VLMs) (Qwen Team, 2026; Bai et al., 2025a; Comanici et al., 2025) have shown strong capabilities in understanding and reasoning over complex video content. However, long-video question answering (VQA) is still largely handled by fitting as much video input as possible into a finite context width. A long video may contain thousands of frames, while the answer to a question is often supported by only a compact set of moments (Tang et al., 2025b; Wang et al., 2025a). Processing the full video at high resolution provides broad temporal coverage and fine visual detail, but the visual-token cost quickly becomes too large. Uniformly sampling fewer frames reduces this cost, but it may miss the short event, object state, or scene change that determines the answer. Token budget matters not only because fewer visual tokens reduce inference cost, but also because context width is a hard capacity limit. On common 24–48GB GPUs, an 8B VLM can often handle only tens of thousands of tokens before memory and latency become limiting. Long-video QA therefore needs methods that operate within a fixed context width, not only methods that reduce average cost. Token-compression methods reduce visual redundancy through pruning, merging, or compression inside the model (Yao et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025), but they often require architectural changes or access to internal token states. Adaptive frame selection uses relevance, coverage, attention, or inference feedback to construct compact evidence sets (Tang et al., 2025a; Chen et al., 2026b; Wang et al., 2026b; Eltahir et al., 2026). These signals offer efficient selection, while leaving open how to train explicit evidence requests for the downstream question. Agentic video-observation methods plan and refine evidence acquisition through repeated or parallel tool calls (Li et al., 2026b; Xu et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026c). We study how best to use a global video preview to guide the acquisition of question-relevant detail under a limited context budget. Specifically, we explore densely sampled Video Previews (VPre) at four spatial resolutions τlo = 1 , 6, 12 , and 24 

visual tokens per frame (Figure 1, 2). Even when fine details are lost, low-resolution frames often preserve coarse cues such as scene layout, object presence, motion, and event boundaries. These cues may be insufficient for final answer verifica-tion, but they can help the model decide where to spend high-resolution tokens. We formulate this setting as token-budgeted Video Evidence Indexing (VEI). Given a densely sampled low-resolution VPre, the model performs VEI by map-ping the preview and question to a compact Evi-dence Set (EvSet) under a fixed token budget. The goal is not to find a single salient keyframe, but to construct a question-relevant set of high-resolution frames that supports final reasoning while balanc-ing coverage, diversity, and redundancy reduction. Our formulation turns VQA into a policy-learning problem. A pretrained VLM has general visual reasoning ability, but it is not specifically trained to allocate a limited visual budget from a blurry preview. A strong VEI policy must jointly solve evidence localization , which identifies question-relevant moments, and budget planning , which decides where limited high-resolution frames should be spent. However, most VQA datasets provide answer labels without corresponding frame-level evidence annotations, making direct supervised fine-tuning (SFT) (Zhang et al., 2025d) difficult. Reinforcement learning with answer-level rewards, such as GRPO (Shao et al., 2024), can optimize final-answer correctness, but provides little direct guidance for individual selection decisions. A correct answer alone does not validate the preceding evidence choices, so even successful rollouts cannot reliably serve as reference labels for evidence localization and budget planning. To guide these intermediate decisions without frame-level labels, we adopt privileged self-distillation (Zhao et al., 2026). A frozen teacher receives the video preview and correct answer, and is instructed to identify visible evidence supporting that answer. This provides pseudo-supervision for evidence localization and budget planning: the teacher can generate reference indexing traces for the student to learn from through off-policy distillation. In practice, we let the student generate 2its own indexing traces while the teacher supplies token-level guidance along them, following prior work (Agarwal et al., 2024). This on-policy setting, also favored by our ablations, aligns supervision with the decisions the student actually encounters, teaching it what evidence to seek and how to distribute its limited observations. Our contributions are summarized as follows: • We explore the use of dense Video Previews for long-video question answering across four preview token budgets, demonstrating their potential to guide evidence acquisition under limited context. • We formulate Video Evidence Indexing (VEI) as a policy that couples evidence localization with budget planning, using explicit observation targets and target-wise timestamp selection to acquire complementary evidence. • We address missing frame-level labels by deriving supervision for VEI decisions from an answer-aware teacher. Privileged self-distillation transfers this guidance across preview resolutions. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Efficient video representations and memory. Token pruning and merging reduce visual redun-dancy (Yao et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025; Du et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2026), while learned compressors and reusable memories retain compact video context (Fei et al., 2026; Yamao et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026a; Cai et al., 2026). AdaCodec encodes detailed reference frames and compact inter-frame changes (Hou et al., 2026); MACF distributes segment perception across agents communicating through latent messages (Chen et al., 2026a). These approaches compress representations or aggregate observations. VEI instead generates explicit evidence requests from a visual preview and reloads selected frames, preserving global coverage while reserving detailed visual tokens for question-relevant evidence. 

Frame selection and budget allocation. Encoder-based selectors use question relevance, coverage, or diversity (Tang et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2025c; 2026b; Chen et al., 2026b; Shi et al., 2026); MEC constructs a ranking reusable across budgets. Other approaches use confidence-guided explo-ration (Zhu et al., 2026b), timestamped textual grounding (Wei et al., 2026), or learned contextual selectors (Buch et al., 2025; Qin et al., 2026). DAFS extracts frame scores from VLM attention and allocates candidate and resolution budgets (Wang et al., 2026b), while EcoFrame and GridProbe use inference feedback or multi-frame confidence probes (Li et al., 2026a; Eltahir et al., 2026). ReUnit allocates mixed-granularity visual layouts (Tang et al., 2026). VEI uses a dense visual preview to formulate explicit observation targets and distribute timestamps across them. This lets the policy allocate its limited frames according to what each target requires, with the aim of covering the different evidence needed to answer the question. 

Reasoning-driven video acquisition. Agentic methods reason about which evidence to inspect through temporal tools and adaptive observation (Zhang et al., 2025b; Xie et al., 2025; He et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2025; Ding et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025; Jin et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025b). LensWalk plans temporal scope and sampling density, while ReViSe maintains a compact state across rounds (Li et al., 2026b; Xu et al., 2026). A.I.R. and VideoXAgent further adapt evidence acquisition to the question (Zou et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2026b). ParaVT learns parallel video-tool calls with reinforcement learning (Yang et al., 2026c); AutoSkill routes questions to discovered selection programs (Hu et al., 2026). VEI plans all evidence requests from one dense preview before answering, avoiding repeated search rounds while keeping the high-resolution frame budget fixed. 

Privileged visual self-distillation. On-policy distillation supervises student-generated pre-fixes (Agarwal et al., 2024); self-distillation can derive guidance from richer teacher context (Zhao et al., 2026; Ye et al., 2026; Hübotter et al., 2026). Video-OPSD and Clue-OPSD use annotated evidence frames or intervals to strengthen video supervision (Wang et al., 2026c;a). RP-OPSD, S2VOPD, and Vision-OPD exploit resolution, augmentation, or regional-view asymmetries (Zhu et al., 2026a; Li et al., 2026c; Yuan et al., 2026). These methods primarily distill perception and answer reasoning from visual inputs. VEI distills the evidence-acquisition policy, teaching the model what to inspect and how to distribute high-resolution budget. ## 3 METHOD 

We study multiple-choice long-video QA under a fixed visual-token budget B. Given a video V , a question Q, and answer choices A = {a1, . . . , a M }, the model must acquire the evidence needed to answer within this budget. As shown in Figure 1, our inference pipeline follows four steps: Video Preview, Video Evidence Indexing, Evidence Set construction, and Answer Generation. The VEI policy performs two coupled behaviors: evidence localization identifies what visual evidence to look for, and budget planning decides where to spend the limited high-resolution frames. We then train these behaviors through privileged self-distillation. 3.1 VIDEO PREVIEW

Among the tested objectives, our ablation favors reverse KL over forward KL and generalized Jensen–Shannon divergence (Section 4.5). The same objective provides guidance for both VEI behaviors. ## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

Backbone and variants. We use Qwen3-VL-8B (Bai et al., 2025a) as the backbone model. 

Training-Free VEI denotes Training-Free Video Evidence Indexing, which uses the VEI infer-ence pipeline without parameter updates. Self-Distilled VEI denotes Self-Distilled Video Evidence Indexing, which trains the preview-based indexing policy with privileged self-distillation and uses the trained checkpoint for both indexing and answering at test time. 

Datasets. We evaluate on VideoMME, LVBench, and MLVU (Fu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025a; Zhou et al., 2025). VideoMME contains videos from roughly one minute to one hour. LVBench focuses on long videos and provides temporal evidence windows for localization analysis. For MLVU, we evaluate on the 502-question multiple-choice test set. We report percentage accuracy. 

Inference protocol and settings. For low-budget settings, the model first samples the video at 

1 fps and compresses each preview frame to a fixed visual-token cap τlo ∈ { 1, 6, 12 , 24 }. This dense low-resolution input serves as the Video Preview. We reduce the preview sampling rate only when required by the context limit. For VEI methods, the model indexes question-relevant evidence timestamps from the Video Preview (VPre), and the selected Evidence Set (EvSet) is then re-injected at higher resolution for final answer prediction. The uniform baseline uses the same VPre and high-resolution frame budget, replacing EvSet with uniformly sampled frames. Unless otherwise stated, the additional EvSet budget is 32 frames with a maximum of 128 visual tokens per frame. The full-context Qwen3-VL baseline uses at most 2048 frames and 640 tokens per frame, the default setting in the original report. The preview budgets τlo ∈ { 1, 6, 12 , 24 } are chosen to cover a wide range of compression levels while avoiding distorted aspect ratios from very small intermediate token grids. For example, 2 tokens can force a 1:2 or 2:1 grid, which distorts many videos. For evaluation, we use deterministic decoding with temperature 0.0, repetition penalty 2.0, thinking disabled, and a maximum generation length of 1024 tokens. Our evaluations use a single run per setting. 

Self-distillation training. Self-Distilled VEI is trained on the MLVU training set. To fit training into available GPU memory, we keep videos shorter than 20 minutes, yielding 2044 multiple-choice questions and 495 short-answer questions. The frozen teacher uses 24 visual tokens per preview frame and receives the correct answer, supplemented by the question type when available. For each example, the student uses a preview budget sampled uniformly from {1, 6, 12 , 24 } visual tokens per frame, without privileged information. We train for 1,500 steps with a batch size of 8 using full-vocabulary reverse-KL distillation. Training uses LoRA on four H100 GPUs and takes 40 hours. Evidence recall measures the fraction of evidence seconds covered within δ of a selection; evidence precision measures the fraction of selections within δ seconds of the evidence window. Evidence F1 is their harmonic mean; these three metrics are averaged per question. Recall is central here because it measures how much supporting evidence is recovered. s, self-distillation raises recall from 14 .1% to 16 .9% , supporting improved evidence coverage as an explanation for the QA gain from highly compressed previews. Table 5 uses the privileged teacher as an empirical upper reference for answer-aware localization. Self-Distilled VEI approaches the teacher’s hit rate and evidence recall, and matches its answer accuracy on hit cases without privileged inputs. Across all three policies, hits are associated with substantially higher answer accuracy, but do not guarantee correctness. 4.5 PRIVILEGED SELF -D ISTILLATION ANALYSIS 

Privileged guidance. The teacher diagnostic in Table 5 reveals the evidence selections available under answer-aware conditioning; final answering remains non-privileged. During training, the teacher instead scores the student’s prefixes as described in Section 3.3, transferring this guidance without supplying a separate gold trace or frame-level labels. 

Rollout policy and objective. Figure 5 compares student-generated traces with teacher-generated traces. Student rollouts perform better in most settings, consistent with the benefit of supervising prefixes the student actually produces. This matters for VEI because later timestamps depend on earlier target choices. Reverse KL gives the strongest result in this comparison, followed by forward KL, while the JSD variants are slightly lower. We therefore use student rollouts and reverse KL for privileged self-distillation. The multi-resolution training setting additionally exposes one student to all four preview resolutions, while the teacher retains its 24-token preview. 

## 5 CONCLUSION 

We explore how dense, compressed Video Previews can guide evidence acquisition for long-video QA under limited context. VEI couples evidence localization with budget planning to construct a compact, question-relevant Evidence Set. We adopt privileged self-distillation to learn these decisions without frame-level annotations, producing one policy across four preview resolutions, including one token per frame. ## A COMPONENT ABLATION WITH COMPARABLE SELECTION COUNTS 

The full-test-set ablation in Table 4 gives every method 32 evidence frames, but VEI supplies more of these through its own selections and requires fewer uniform additions. To examine whether its advantage persists when this difference is smaller, we filter the same 502 MLVU questions by the number of frames each method selects. 

Selection rule. For each question, we count each method’s valid, unique selections before uniform filling and cap this count at 32. We retain the question if the capped counts for Base FS and + EL are each within ±3 frames of + EL + BP (VEI). Thus, questions where all three independently select at least 32 frames automatically qualify. The original answering protocol is unchanged: excess selections are subsampled and shortages are uniformly supplemented to obtain exactly 32 evidence frames. ## D ADDITIONAL DISCUSSION : E VIDENCE INDEXING 

Long-video Video Evidence Indexing differs from standard keyframe sampling. In many QA examples, evidence may be spread across multiple moments: an object appears early, an action happens later, and the answer depends on comparing them. VEI therefore treats the preview-based indexing phase as Evidence Set construction, not isolated keyframe selection. The target list helps distribute the high-resolution frame budget across different evidence needs, while privileged self-distillation helps train this missing policy skill from student-generated rollouts.

## H LIMITATIONS 

Our evaluation focuses on a small set of 8B VLMs, and results may vary with model scale and pretraining. We report single-run evaluations without estimates of training-seed variability. Broader adaptive-selector comparisons would extend our uniform, CLIP-retrieval, and nested-VEI controls. Our CLIP † baseline uses a publicly pretrained CLIP model to embed the question and each candidate frame independently. We rank frames by question–image embedding similarity and select the top 32, retaining the same preview and high-resolution evidence budget as Training-Free VEI. The localization analysis measures temporal evidence recovery; direct measurements of target coverage and visual redundancy remain outside this study. The distilled checkpoint performs both indexing and answering, so the QA results characterize the complete pipeline without separately quantifying changes in answering behavior. Very low-resolution previews can obscure evidence needed for indexing, while a fixed-size Evidence Set may under-cover extended events. VEI processes completed videos and does not address causal online streaming. Our efficiency analysis focuses on context capacity; end-to-end latency and memory across both inference calls remain to be characterized. 21
