ABSTRACT 

Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) achieve strong performance across vision-language tasks, yet the internal mechanisms by which visual and textual information are fused across lay-ers remain insufficiently understood. We investigate representative MLLMs from two architectural paradigms: concatenation architectures and native multimodal architectures. We conduct three pro-gressively connected analyses: alignment decoupling identifies which modality changes, attention routing and entropy characterize how cross-modal information is distributed, and intrinsic dimen-sionality examines how fusion reshapes feature spaces. Separately, we perform causal intervention experiments as a validation of the resulting interpretation. As a supplementary analysis, we use vi-sual CKA to examine the Platonic Representation Hypothesis. Together, these analyses reveal two distinct fusion pathways: concatenation models follow a text-first, vision-later pathway, whereas na-tive models exhibit earlier visual-textual co-adaptation and feature-space reorganization. This work provides a mechanistic perspective for understanding multimodal fusion and supports architecture-aware diagnostics of multimodal representations. 

1 Introduction 

MLLMs have rapidly advanced a wide range of vision-language applications, including human-computer interac-tion [1], medical image analysis [2], and autonomous driving [3]. Despite strong empirical progress, most studies still evaluate MLLMs primarily through downstream task accuracy and multimodal reasoning benchmarks [4, 5]. Fi-nal performance, however, does not reveal how visual and textual tokens interact inside the model. The question is therefore not only whether an MLLM solves a task, but also how it fuses modalities across depth. A central factor behind this problem is architectural diversity. Existing MLLMs can be broadly categorized into two paradigms according to how visual and textual information are integrated [6]: concatenation architectures and native multimodal architectures . We refer to Section 2.1 for the architectural definitions and representative examples. Al-though these paradigms differ in how they introduce and process visual information, their layer-wise fusion dynamics have not been systematically characterized. Prior work has revealed modality gaps in multimodal representation learning [7], shown that attention mechanisms shape multimodal representations [8], and demonstrated that neural representations evolve substantially across lay-ers [9, 10]. Motivated by these findings, we first conduct a layer-wise cross-modal representation analysis using Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) [11]. CKA measures the similarity between two feature matrices and is widely used for comparison in multimodal analysis because it is robust to orthogonal transformations and isotropic scaling. As illustrated in Figure 1, concatenation architectures generally exhibit relatively high similarity in early layers, al-though the magnitude depends on the employed vision encoder. These models then experience a pronounced decline in intermediate layers, suggesting a temporary divergence between modalities during feature transformation. 2 PRELIMINARY 

2.1 MLLM Architectures 

MLLMs can generally be divided into concatenation architectures and native multimodal architectures according to the design of the vision-language interface [6]. Concatenation architectures preserve the original language model backbone while introducing lightweight projection modules to align visual features with the textual embedding space. Most of these frameworks rely on pretrained CLIP visual encoders [13], whose large-scale contrastive pretraining provides transferable semantic representations for multimodal alignment. The core assumption of such architectures is that the visual encoder already provides sufficiently high-level semantics for visual tokens [14]. Native multimodal architectures learn visual tokens and language representations within a shared end-to-end multi-modal framework. Their visual front ends may be jointly optimized or replaced by encoder-free visual tokenization, so the category is defined by the multimodal learning interface rather than by a single tokenization implementation. Our work is complementary: rather than treating the empirical difference between architectures as self-evident, we examine the internal mechanisms that give rise to different fusion pathways. 

2.2 MLLM Alignment and Interpretability 

Recent works often adopt the heuristic that stronger alignment leads to better multimodal performance [15, 16, 17]. However, recent evidence suggests that stronger alignment does not always lead to better performance [18]. Other studies analyze internal information flow in MLLMs but remain focused primarily on concatenation architectures [19] or on representation alignment alone [20]. Existing probing-based analyses often focus on isolated layer represen-tations rather than the complete evolution of multimodal fusion [9]. In summary, prior work has not yet provided a comprehensive explanation of how different MLLM architectures fuse modalities across depth.

Model Selection. We evaluate three concatenation models, LLaVA-next with Vicuna and Mistral backbones [21] and Cambrian [22], and three native multimodal models, SOLO [23], EVE [24], and SAIL [25]. The LLaVA series uses CLIP-ViT-L [13] as its visual encoder, whereas Cambrian is a distinct concatenation model employing a hybrid visual encoder that integrates DINO [26] and CLIP [13]. Because the two architectural paradigms use fundamentally different training pipelines, a perfectly controlled comparison that fixes every factor is not feasible. Concatenation models typically combine a pretrained visual encoder, a projection module, and a language model backbone, while native models learn visual tokenization and language representations in an end-to-end multimodal framework with model-specific supervision pipelines. Our goal is to identify paradigm-level regularities under representative open models. To reduce single-model artifacts, we evaluate multiple models per paradigm and require the same interpretation to be supported across CKA, attention, intrinsic-dimensionality, and intervention evidence. 

Dataset and Input Construction. We randomly sample 10,000 image-text pairs from COCO-val [27], VQA [28], and MMMU [29] to cover different levels of visual complexity, textual semantic density, and multimodal reasoning requirements. For the representation and attention analyses, we use clean caption-style image-text inputs to reduce noise from long reasoning chains or text spans that are only weakly grounded in the image. All special tokens, padding tokens, and truncation positions are recorded and masked during analysis to ensure that the measured statistics reflect only effective visual and textual tokens. We evaluate complex reasoning behavior separately through the causal perturbation experiment on MMBench [30] using VLMEvalKit [31]. 

Feature and Attention Extraction. For each model and each input sample, we extract the post-norm hidden rep-resentation matrix H(l) ∈ RNl×d from every layer, where Nl denotes the number of valid tokens and d denotes the hidden dimension. In all experiments, the analyzed hidden representations are normalized layer by layer and use a common hidden size of d = 4096 . The numbers of visual and textual tokens follow the default configuration of each model, so the analysis preserves the native tokenization and visual-token construction of different architectures. Ac-cording to the token indices of each model, we separate the hidden states into visual and textual token sets, denoted as H(l)

3.4 Intrinsic Dimensionality Transition Motivation. Both CKA and attention statistics are relative measures. CKA describes similarity between represen-tations, and attention describes how tokens interact, but neither directly indicates whether the feature space is being compressed, expanded, or reorganized during fusion. For example, a decrease in CKA may correspond either to infor-mation loss, feature specialization, or expansion into a richer semantic subspace. To address this limitation, we analyze the intrinsic dimensionality of visual and textual representations. This provides an absolute measure of the effective complexity of the feature space and answers the third mechanistic question: what kind of structural transformation occurs during multimodal fusion?

Because the hidden representations are normalized layer by layer and compared at a fixed hidden size, the intrinsic-dimensionality curves are not caused by differences in hidden size or activation scale across layers. Results. Figure 5 shows that intrinsic dimensionality provides a structural view of the fusion process. For the concatenation model Cambrian, the visual intrinsic dimension remains relatively low and stable through the early and transition ranges. This plateau suggests that the visual tokens initially preserve the compact structure inherited from the pretrained visual encoder. Around the middle-to-deep transition, the visual intrinsic dimension increases, indicating that visual representations expand into a richer effective subspace. The textual intrinsic dimension decreases from its input-side value and then changes more gradually, suggesting early reorganization of the language stream followed by stabilization. For the native model EVE, the visual intrinsic dimension begins to increase from earlier layers and does not exhibit the long shallow-layer plateau observed in concatenation models. The textual intrinsic dimension changes together with the visual side and can peak around the transition before decreasing in later layers. Therefore, native models are better characterized as performing early feature-space expansion and reorganization, rather than strictly monotonic expansion across all layers.

We therefore conduct two complementary causal validations. The second injects Gaussian noise into early-layer or late-layer hidden states and evaluates general multimodal question answering on MMBench using VLMEvalKit [30, 31]. 3.6 Mechanistic Summary 

The three progressive analyses provide an explanation for the fusion. Alignment decoupling shows that concatenation architectures follow a sequential pathway: textual representations adapt earlier, while visual representations remain relatively stable in shallow layers and are reshaped later. Within layers 8–15, attention routing shows that concatenation models allocate substantial visual-query attention to textual keys, with lower normalized entropy in this direction than in the reverse direction. Intrinsic dimensionality reveals the structural nature of this process: the later deformation of visual representations corresponds to delayed feature-space expansion. Therefore, concatenation models can be summarized as following a text-first, vision-later fusion pathway. Native architectures exhibit a different pattern. Their visual representations change from earlier layers, and textual representations also evolve with depth. The attention summaries show model-specific directional asymmetries, in-cluding more diffuse visual-to-text attention in EVE and SOLO than in the concatenation models, with SAIL forming a structural exception documented in Appendix A.3. Their intrinsic dimensionality curves suggest earlier feature-space expansion and reorganization, and their stronger sensitivity to early perturbation supports the view that fusion begins earlier. Thus, the native-model results support earlier visual-textual co-adaptation while also revealing heterogeneous attention profiles. Separately, the causal intervention results support the functional relevance of these two pathway interpretations.

5 Conclusion 

The main contribution of this work is to uncover internal fusion mechanisms and design experiments to validate them. The findings can suggest several concrete directions. First, transition layers and sparse textual anchors can be used as robustness diagnostics, especially for studying adversarial vulnerability and hallucination behavior. Second, training strategies for concatenation models may benefit from earlier cross-modal feedback, anchor regularization, or layer-selective adaptation around the transition band. Finally, layer-selective fine-tuning, adapters, or LoRA modules can be guided by the observed pathway differences instead of being placed uniformly across all layers.

A.4 Intrinsic Dimension 

The intrinsic dimensionality analysis in the main text characterizes whether multimodal fusion corresponds to feature-space compression, expansion, or reorganization. Here, we provide the original per-model visual and textual curves for additional models, followed by a threshold-sensitivity analysis for the two representative models used in the main paper. As shown in Figure 10, concatenation models generally show delayed visual dimensionality expansion, while native models show earlier and more continuous feature-space growth. This is consistent with the main observation that concatenation models follow a text-first, vision-later fusion pathway, whereas native models perform earlier modality co-adaptation. Each model’s plot contains multiple curves corresponding to different variance retention thresholds for the sensitivity analysis. The trends of all models remain consistent across varying thresholds, indicating that the observed patterns are not caused by a specific variance cutoff. 

A.5 Cross-model Visual Representation Similarity 

To further examine representation convergence across different MLLMs, we compute pairwise visual CKA similarity between models at shallow and deep layers. This supplementary experiment is motivated by the Platonic Representa-tion Hypothesis [12]: from the perspective of representation similarity, if different MLLMs gradually converge toward similar high-level visual semantics, their deep-layer visual representations should become more aligned. As shown in Figure 11, visual representations are strongly architecture-dependent in shallow layers. Models with similar visual processing strategies show higher similarity, while cross-paradigm similarities remain relatively low. In contrast, deeper-layer heatmaps show substantially higher similarity across model pairs. This result suggests that although different MLLMs start from different visual representation spaces, their deep visual representations become more aligned after multimodal fusion. A.6 Bootstrap Robustness Analysis 

To quantify sampling uncertainty and evaluate robustness, we perform non-parametric bootstrap resampling. We use a 5,000-pair analysis population, define four sample-size groups of 500, 1,000, 2,000, and 5,000 pairs, and conduct 1,000 independent random resamplings with replacement for each group, using image-text pairs as the resampling unit. Layers 1, 15, and 30 are selected as representative layers, and the bootstrap mean under the 5,000-sample condition is used as the reference estimate for computing relative bias. For layer l, relative bias (%) is 100(¯ cn,l − ¯c5000 ,l )/¯c5000 ,l ,where ¯cn,l is the bootstrap mean for sample size n. It measures deviation from the 5,000-pair reference estimate, rather than bias relative to an unknown population CKA value. All stochastic processes use a fixed random seed. Figure 12 visualizes the relative-bias trend, while Table 3 reports the exact Cambrian CKA values. The standard errors remain small across all sample sizes, and the relative bias decreases as the sample size increases.
